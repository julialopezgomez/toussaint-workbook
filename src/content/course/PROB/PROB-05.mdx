## Why this matters

Gaussians are the workhorse distribution of robotics and ML: Kalman filters, Gaussian processes (you met these in [OPT-06](/course/OPT/OPT-06)), variational inference, and the Laplace approximation (next module) all lean on the specific algebraic identities this module covers. Because these identities get used constantly but are easy to misremember under pressure, this module is deliberately reference-style, expect to come back to it.

## The Gaussian density, formally

> **Definition (Gaussian, 1D).** In one dimension, a Gaussian distribution is defined by mean $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ and variance $\sigma^2\in\mathbb{R}$:
> $$N(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{|2\pi\sigma^2|^{1/2}}\,e^{-\frac12(x-\mu)^2/\sigma^2}$$
> $\sigma$ is called the **standard deviation**. The **$z$-score** of a value $x$ is $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$, its distance from the mean measured in units of standard deviation.

> **Definition (Gaussian, $n$ dimensions).** In $n$ dimensions, a Gaussian is defined by mean $a\in\mathbb{R}^n$ and symmetric positive-definite covariance matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ ([MATH-03B](/course/MATH/MATH-03B)'s formal PD definition applies directly here, a covariance matrix *must* be PD):
> $$N(x\mid a,A) = \frac{1}{|2\pi A|^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac12(x-a)^\top A^{-1}(x-a)\right\}$$

<ExerciseCard id="PROB-05-ex0-density-eval" />

There's also a **canonical form**, with symmetric positive-definite **precision matrix** $A$ (note: the same symbol $A$ now plays a different role, precision, not covariance, only its context/bracket-type tells you which):

$$N[x\mid a,A] = \frac{\exp\{-\tfrac12a^\top A^{-1}a\}}{|2\pi A^{-1}|^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\tfrac12x^\top Ax+x^\top a\right\}$$

with the conversion $N[x\mid a,A]=N(x\mid A^{-1}a,A^{-1})$ (equivalently $N(x\mid a,A)=N[x\mid A^{-1}a,A^{-1}]$): moment-form mean $=$ precision $\times$ canonical-form "mean parameter," moment-form covariance $=$ inverse of canonical-form precision. The canonical form looks less intuitive at first, but it's the form in which **products of Gaussians add directly** (below), which is precisely why it exists as a named, separate convention rather than just being "the same thing written differently for no reason."

## The product of two Gaussians

<ExerciseCard id="PROB-05-ex2-product-derivation" />

The full result (mean and variance both) for 1D Gaussians $N(x\mid a,A)$, $N(x\mid b,B)$:

$$\sigma_c^2 = \left(\frac1A+\frac1B\right)^{-1}, \qquad \mu_c = \sigma_c^2\left(\frac{a}{A}+\frac{b}{B}\right)$$

This is a **precision-weighted average**: the combined mean leans toward whichever input has the tighter (smaller) variance, i.e. more confidence. The multivariable generalization replaces $1/A$ with $A^{-1}$ throughout (matrix inverse instead of scalar reciprocal), and the general product identity is most naturally stated in canonical form (precisions really do just add):

$$N(x\mid a,A)\,N(x\mid b,B) = N[x\mid A^{-1}a+B^{-1}b,\,A^{-1}+B^{-1}]\cdot N(a\mid b,A+B)$$

(the trailing $N(a\mid b,A+B)$ factor is a normalizing constant, not depending on $x$, needed since the product of two normalized densities isn't itself normalized in general.)

## Worked example

$N(x\mid 2,1)$ and $N(x\mid 4,2)$: $\sigma_c^2 = (1/1+1/2)^{-1} = 0.6\overline{6}$, $\mu_c = 0.6\overline{6}(2/1+4/2) = 0.6\overline{6}(4) \approx 2.667$. *(Verified with NumPy while authoring this module.)* The combined mean sits closer to $2$ than to $4$, since the first Gaussian ($A=1$) is more confident (tighter) than the second ($B=2$).

## Marginal and conditional of a joint Gaussian

For a jointly-Gaussian $(x,y)$ with mean $(a,b)$ and covariance $\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\C^\top&B\end{pmatrix}$, the **marginal** of $x$ is simply $N(x\mid a,A)$ (drop the rows/columns for $y$ entirely), and the **conditional** is:

$$N\left(\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\C^\top&B\end{pmatrix}\right) = N(x\mid a,A)\cdot N\big(y\mid b+C^\top A^{-1}(x-a),\ B-C^\top A^{-1}C\big)$$

i.e. observing $x$ shifts $y$'s mean by a term proportional to the cross-covariance $C^\top$ and the "surprise" $(x-a)$, and *shrinks* $y$'s variance by $C^\top A^{-1}C$ (which is PSD, so the conditional variance is never larger than the marginal, observing a correlated variable can only reduce uncertainty, never increase it). **This is exactly the Kalman filter's update step.**

## Reference: further Gaussian identities

The source note collects a larger set of identities than this module derives in full; these are worth knowing exist so you can look them up precisely when needed, rather than re-deriving them each time:

- **Convolution**: $\int_x N(x\mid a,A)\,N(y-x\mid b,B)\,dx = N(y\mid a+b, A+B)$, variances add under convolution.
- **Linear transformation**: $N(Fx+f\mid a,A) = \frac{1}{|F|}N(x\mid F^{-1}(a-f),\,F^{-1}AF^{-\top})$, needed whenever you push a Gaussian belief through a linear map (e.g. a linearized dynamics model).
- **Expectations**: $\mathbb{E}_x\{x\}=a$, $\mathbb{E}_x\{xx^\top\}=A+aa^\top$.
- **Entropy**: $H(N(a,A)) = \tfrac12\log|2\pi eA|$.
- **KL divergence between two Gaussians**: $2D(N(a,A)\|N(b,B)) = \log\frac{|B|}{|A|}+\text{tr}(B^{-1}A)+(b-a)^\top B^{-1}(b-a)-n$, a closed form in terms of $a,A,b,B$ (no sampling needed).

## Summary

- A Gaussian is formally $N(x\mid a,A)=\frac{1}{|2\pi A|^{1/2}}\exp\{-\tfrac12(x-a)^\top A^{-1}(x-a)\}$, with covariance $A$ required to be symmetric positive-definite (MATH-03B); the canonical form $N[x\mid a,A]$ uses a precision matrix instead.
- Gaussians are closed under products, marginals, and conditionals, the property that makes sequential Bayesian filtering with fixed-size representations possible.
- Product of two Gaussians: precisions add ($1/\sigma_c^2 = 1/A+1/B$); the combined mean is a precision-weighted average.
- The conditional of a joint Gaussian shifts the mean by a cross-covariance-weighted correction and shrinks the variance, exactly the Kalman filter update.
- A fuller reference set of identities (convolution, linear transformation, expectations, entropy, KL) exists in the source note for lookup as needed.
