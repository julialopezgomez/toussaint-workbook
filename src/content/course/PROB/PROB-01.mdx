## Why this matters

Probability is the language every uncertainty-aware method in this course speaks: Bayesian optimization, state estimation, learned probabilistic models, and the sampling-based planning methods central to your own research all reduce to careful bookkeeping with joint/marginal/conditional distributions and Bayes' rule. This module rebuilds that bookkeeping precisely, since sloppy probability reasoning is a common, hard-to-notice source of bugs.

## Formal definitions: probability measure and random variable

> **Definition (probability measure).** The **sample space** (or domain) $\Omega$ is the space of possible outcomes of an experiment (e.g. $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ for a die). A probability measure is a mapping $P:A\subseteq\Omega\mapsto[0,1]$ such that:
> - $p(A)\ge0$ for any $A\subseteq\Omega$ (**non-negativity**);
> - $p(\bigcup_iA_i)=\sum_ip(A_i)$ if $A_i\cap A_j=\emptyset$ (**additivity**, for disjoint sets);
> - $p(\Omega)=1$ (**normalization**).

From just these three axioms, several familiar facts follow directly: $0\le p(A)\le1$; $p(\emptyset)=0$; $A\subseteq B\Rightarrow p(A)\le p(B)$; $p(A\cup B)=p(A)+p(B)-p(A\cap B)$ (inclusion-exclusion, needed once $A,B$ aren't disjoint); $p(\Omega\setminus A)=1-p(A)$.

> **Definition (random variable).** For a discrete domain $\Omega$, $p(X{=}x)\in\mathbb{R}$ denotes the specific probability that $X=x$; $p(X)$ denotes the whole probability *distribution* (a function over $\Omega$), with $\sum_{x\in\Omega}p(X{=}x)=1$. Formally, a random variable is a mapping $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ from the sample space to $\mathbb{R}$ (or another measurable space); this pushes the probability measure onto $\mathbb{R}$: $p(X\in A)=p(\{\omega:X(\omega)\in A\})$.

For a die, $\Omega=\{1,..,6\}$ and $p(X)$ is literally "the probability table," a single number for each outcome. The same machinery extends directly to **continuous** distributions, replacing $\sum_x$ with $\int dx$: e.g. $\Omega=\mathbb{R}$, $p(x{=}1)=1.5$ is a **density** value (not itself a probability, densities can exceed 1), with $\int_{-\infty}^\infty p(x)\,dx=1$.

## Joint, marginal, conditional

> **Definitions (joint, marginal, conditional).** For two random variables $X,Y$: $p(X,Y)$ denotes their **joint distribution**. Based on this, define the **marginal** $p(X)=\sum_Yp(X,Y)$, and the **conditional** $p(X\mid Y)=\dfrac{p(X,Y)}{p(Y)}$, which satisfies $\forall Y:\sum_Xp(X\mid Y)=1$.
>
> $X$ is **independent** of $Y$ iff $p(X,Y)=p(X)p(Y)$, or equivalently, $p(X\mid Y)=p(X)$.

## Product rule and Bayes' rule

From the two definitions above, $p(X)=\sum_Yp(X,Y)$ and $p(X\mid Y)=p(X,Y)/p(Y)$, it follows directly that

$$p(X,Y) = p(X\mid Y)\,p(Y) = p(Y\mid X)\,p(X) \qquad\text{(product rule)}$$

$$p(X\mid Y) = \frac{p(Y\mid X)\,p(X)}{p(Y)} \qquad\text{(Bayes' rule)}$$

The individual terms in Bayes' rule have standard names, worth having automatic since you'll see this vocabulary constantly (Bayesian optimization, Bayesian filtering, Bayesian inference generally):

$$\underbrace{p(X\mid Y)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(Y\mid X)}^{\text{likelihood}}\cdot\overbrace{p(X)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(Y)}_{\text{evidence}}}$$

<ExerciseCard id="PROB-01-ex2-bayes-derivation" />

**Stop and try**: before continuing, notice that Bayes' rule is really just "the same joint probability, factored two different ways, set equal to each other." This is the same trick you'll see again when the Lagrangian's two forms of stationarity condition line up, or when a joint Gaussian's marginal and conditional turn out to be consistent (PROB-05): a recurring theme in this course is exploiting the fact that one quantity can be computed multiple ways, and equating those ways.

## Worked example: the base-rate effect

A classic, important illustration of Bayes' rule: a diagnostic test with prior disease prevalence $P(\text{disease})=0.01$, true-positive rate $P(\text{pos}\mid\text{disease})=0.95$, false-positive rate $P(\text{pos}\mid\text{no disease})=0.05$.

$$P(\text{pos}) = 0.95(0.01) + 0.05(0.99) = 0.0095+0.0495 = 0.059$$

$$P(\text{disease}\mid\text{pos}) = \frac{0.95(0.01)}{0.059} \approx 0.161$$

Despite a 95% accurate test, a positive result only means about a 16% chance of actually having the disease, because the disease is rare, so the (much larger) healthy population still contributes more false positives in absolute terms than the small diseased population contributes true positives. *(Verified numerically while authoring this module.)* This "base-rate effect" comes up constantly in any Bayesian reasoning involving a rare prior.

## More variables, and a first look at Bayesian networks

Everything above generalizes directly to $n$ random variables $X_{1:n}$: the joint $p(X_{1:n})$ can (by definition!) always be **factored** via repeated application of the product rule, e.g. $p(X_{1:n})=\prod_{i=1}^np(X_i\mid X_{i+1:n})$, and $X$ is **conditionally independent** of $Y$ given $Z$ iff $p(X\mid Y,Z)=p(X\mid Z)$.

Often, a variable only depends on *some* others, not all of them, which is very useful to know for simplifying computations. A **Bayesian network** formalizes this: a directed acyclic graph where each node is a random variable $X_i$, associated with a conditional distribution $p(X_i\mid\text{Parents}(X_i))$, generalizing the computation-graph idea from [MATH-01](/course/MATH/MATH-01) to probabilistic dependencies. E.g. instead of the fully general $p(X_{1:4})=p(X_1)p(X_2|X_1)p(X_3|X_1,X_2)p(X_4|X_1,X_2,X_3)$, a graph might tell you $p(X_{1:4})=p(X_1)p(X_2|X_1)p(X_3|X_2)p(X_4|X_3)$ instead, far cheaper to represent and compute with. This is background context for later, richer modules; it isn't developed further here.

## Summary

- A probability measure satisfies non-negativity, additivity (on disjoint sets), and normalization; a random variable formally maps outcomes to $\mathbb{R}$.
- Joint $P(X,Y)$ is the complete description; marginal $P(X)=\sum_YP(X,Y)$ sums out $Y$; conditional $P(X\mid Y{=}y)=P(X,y)/P(y)$ slices at $Y{=}y$ and renormalizes.
- Bayes' rule follows directly from writing the same joint two ways via the two conditional-probability definitions; its terms are named posterior/likelihood/prior/evidence.
- A rare prior can make even an accurate test's positive result much less conclusive than it first appears, always check the base rate.
- Bayesian networks generalize the computation-graph idea to probabilistic dependencies, factoring a joint distribution using only the dependencies that actually matter.
