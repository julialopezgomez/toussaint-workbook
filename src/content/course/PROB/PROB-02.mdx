## Why sample at all?

> **Monte Carlo estimation.** A Monte Carlo (MC) method generates samples $x_i\sim p(x)$ from a distribution $p(x)$, then uses them to estimate an expectation of anything depending on $x$:
> $$\mathbb{E}_p\{f(x)\} = \int_xp(x)f(x)\,dx \approx \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Nf(x_i)$$
> A **weighted** variant generates weighted samples $(x_i,w_i)$ with $\sum_iw_i=1$: $\mathbb{E}_p\{f(x)\}\approx\sum_{i=1}^Nw_if(x_i)$.

## Importance sampling

<ExerciseCard id="PROB-02-ex2-importance-sampling-derivation" />

**Worked example**: target $p=[0.1,0.6,0.3]$ over $x\in\{1,2,3\}$ (with values $[1,2,3]$), proposal $q=[0.3,0.3,0.4]$. True mean $\mathbb{E}_p[x] = 0.1(1)+0.6(2)+0.3(3) = 2.2$. Importance weights: $w=[0.333, 2.0, 0.75]$. Checking the identity: $\mathbb{E}_q[x\cdot w(x)] = 0.3(1\cdot0.333)+0.3(2\cdot2.0)+0.4(3\cdot0.75) = 0.1+1.2+0.9 = 2.2$. *(Verified with NumPy while authoring this module: matches exactly, as the derivation guarantees.)*

## Rejection sampling, briefly

An alternative when you can evaluate $p(x)$ pointwise but not sample from it directly: find a constant $M$ such that $p(x)\le Mq(x)$ for all $x$ (using an easy-to-sample proposal $q$), then sample $x\sim q$ and accept it with probability $p(x)/(Mq(x))$, rejecting (and resampling) otherwise. The accepted samples are then genuinely distributed according to $p$. The overall acceptance rate works out to exactly $1/M$, so a tight bound $M$ (as close to the true worst-case ratio $\max_x p(x)/q(x)$ as possible) matters a great deal for efficiency: a loose $M$ wastes most proposed samples on rejection.

## Particle approximation of a distribution

Directly relevant to sampling-based planning: a **particle distribution** represents $p(x)$ over a continuous domain $\mathbb{R}^n$ as a weighted set of $N$ discrete samples ("particles"),

$$q(x) := \sum_{i=1}^Nw_i\,\delta(x-x_i), \qquad \sum_iw_i=1$$

where $\delta(x-x_i)$ is the Dirac delta (a "spike" at $x_i$) and each particle has a location $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^n$ and weight $w_i$. Given such a set, the same weighted Monte Carlo formula from above applies directly: $\mathbb{E}_p\{f(x)\}\approx\sum_iw_if(x_i)$.

<ExerciseCard id="PROB-02-ex4-particle-weighted-expectation" />

This is a genuinely versatile representation (numeric, works for arbitrary, "difficult"/multi-modal distributions with no closed form), at the cost of needing many samples for high-dimensional or high-precision cases. It underlies **particle filters** (state estimation, e.g. robot localization) and **sample-based planning** (Monte Carlo Tree Search, and the sampling-based motion planners directly relevant to your own research), both of which you'll meet again in later blocks: whenever a distribution over states, actions, or trajectories has no tractable closed form, a particle approximation is often the practical fallback.

## Summary

- Sampling replaces symbolic integration with drawing samples and averaging, converging via the law of large numbers, useful whenever exact integration isn't feasible.
- Importance sampling reweights samples from a convenient proposal $q$ by $p(x)/q(x)$ to estimate expectations under a less-convenient target $p$.
- Rejection sampling accepts proposal samples with probability $p(x)/(Mq(x))$; overall acceptance rate is $1/M$, so a tight bound matters for efficiency.
