## Importance sampling

<ExerciseCard id="PROB-02-ex2-importance-sampling-derivation" />

**Worked example**: target $p=[0.1,0.6,0.3]$ over $x\in\{1,2,3\}$ (with values $[1,2,3]$), proposal $q=[0.3,0.3,0.4]$. True mean $\mathbb{E}_p[x] = 0.1(1)+0.6(2)+0.3(3) = 2.2$. Importance weights: $w=[0.333, 2.0, 0.75]$. Checking the identity: $\mathbb{E}_q[x\cdot w(x)] = 0.3(1\cdot0.333)+0.3(2\cdot2.0)+0.4(3\cdot0.75) = 0.1+1.2+0.9 = 2.2$. *(Verified with NumPy while authoring this module: matches exactly, as the derivation guarantees.)*

## Rejection sampling, briefly

An alternative when you can evaluate $p(x)$ pointwise but not sample from it directly: find a constant $M$ such that $p(x)\le Mq(x)$ for all $x$ (using an easy-to-sample proposal $q$), then sample $x\sim q$ and accept it with probability $p(x)/(Mq(x))$, rejecting (and resampling) otherwise. The accepted samples are then genuinely distributed according to $p$. The overall acceptance rate works out to exactly $1/M$, so a tight bound $M$ (as close to the true worst-case ratio $\max_x p(x)/q(x)$ as possible) matters a great deal for efficiency: a loose $M$ wastes most proposed samples on rejection.

## Summary

- Sampling replaces symbolic integration with drawing samples and averaging, converging via the law of large numbers, useful whenever exact integration isn't feasible.
- Importance sampling reweights samples from a convenient proposal $q$ by $p(x)/q(x)$ to estimate expectations under a less-convenient target $p$.
- Rejection sampling accepts proposal samples with probability $p(x)/(Mq(x))$; overall acceptance rate is $1/M$, so a tight bound matters for efficiency.
