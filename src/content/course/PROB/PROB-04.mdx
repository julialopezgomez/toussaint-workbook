## Why this matters

The energy/probability correspondence underlies a huge range of models (Boltzmann machines, energy-based generative models, the Gibbs/Boltzmann connection in statistical mechanics) and reframes "define a probability distribution" as "define any function you like as an energy, exponentiate and normalize." Understanding exactly what's easy (evaluating energy) versus hard (the partition function) about this framing is directly relevant to sampling-based methods in your own research.

## From probability to energy

Given any density $p(x)$, define $E(x) = -\log p(x) + c$ for an arbitrary constant offset $c$. This is the **energy function**. The motivation (from the source note): probabilities are inherently multiplicative (independent events' probabilities multiply), but it's often more convenient to work additively, taking a $-\log$ converts multiplication into addition ($-\log[p(x_1)p(x_2)] = -\log p(x_1) - \log p(x_2)$), and the source shows this specific transform is in fact the *only* way to convert a multiplicative quantity into an additive one, up to the choice of constant.

## The Boltzmann distribution

<ExerciseCard id="PROB-04-ex2-energy-equivalence-derivation" />

Conventionally written with the constant absorbed into a normalizer: $p(x) = \frac1Z\exp(-E(x))$, the **Boltzmann distribution**, with $Z=\int_x\exp(-E(x))\,dx$ the **partition function**.

## Worked example

For a two-state system with $E(x_1)=0$, $E(x_2)=2$: $\exp(-E(x_1))=1$, $\exp(-E(x_2))=\exp(-2)\approx0.1353$, so $Z\approx1.1353$ and $p(x_1) = 1/1.1353 \approx 0.8808$, $p(x_2)\approx0.1192$. *(Verified with NumPy while authoring this module.)* Notice: computing $p(x_1)$ required knowing $Z$, which required evaluating the energy at *every* state, not just $x_1$, exactly the "global" property from the derivation above, made concrete even in this trivial 2-state case.

## Physical origin, briefly

In statistical mechanics, this exact form arises from a system of many particles with additive energies and multiplicative combinatorics: the Boltzmann distribution $p(i)\propto\exp(-\beta E_i)$ describes how likely a single particle is to occupy energy level $i$, with $\beta=1/(k_BT)$ relating temperature to the typical particle energy. In AI/ML contexts, $\beta$ (or equivalently $t=1/\beta$ from the log-barrier method in OPT-04) is usually just a free scaling parameter you choose, not tied to physical temperature.

## Summary

- $E(x)=-\log p(x)+c$ and $p(x)=\frac1Z\exp(-E(x))$ are the same statement, solved for different variables; $Z=\exp(-c)$.
- $E(x)$ is a local, pointwise quantity (easy); $Z$ is a global quantity requiring integration over the whole space (hard).
- Sampling methods using only energy evaluations (e.g. Metropolis-Hastings) are inherently local, risking long mixing times across well-separated modes, precisely because they lack the global information $Z$ would provide.
