## Why this matters

KIN-03 built the forward-kinematics map $q\mapsto\phi(q)$ and solved IK for a single target configuration. Path planning asks a different question: not "what's one good $q$," but "what's the whole *space* of valid $q$'s, and how do I find a route through it?" This module formalizes that space, then surveys the classical heuristics (potential fields, grid search) whose failure modes motivate the sampling-based methods of [PLAN-02](/course/PLAN/PLAN-02).

## Configuration space and feasible configuration space

For a multibody system, the poses $x_{1:m}$ of all bodies fully describe its **configuration**. Introducing coordinates $q\in\mathbb{R}^n$ (the joint vector) for this description, the embedding space of $q$ is the **configuration space** $\mathcal{X}$ (e.g. $\mathcal{X}=SE(3)^m$ generically, or $\mathbb{R}^n$ once coordinates are chosen). Not every $q\in\mathcal{X}$ is physically realizable: joints have limits, and body shapes cannot interpenetrate. The **feasible configuration space** $\mathcal{X}_\text{fea}\subseteq\mathcal{X}$ collects exactly the configurations respecting both. *(Note on terminology: the wider motion-planning literature, e.g. LaValle's* Planning Algorithms*, calls this same set $C_\text{free}$, with $C_\text{obs}:=\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_\text{fea}$ its complement; both names refer to the identical object, and this workbook uses them interchangeably from here on, matching whichever source is being discussed.)*

When a system has $d$ independent **holonomic constraints** $h(q,t)=0$ (a $d$-dimensional constraint function restricting which $q\in\mathcal{X}$ are even kinematically possible, before considering collisions), the generalized coordinates $q\in\mathbb{R}^n$ are not minimal: the true **degrees of freedom** are $p=n-d$. **Minimal coordinates** are generalized coordinates chosen with $n=p$ directly (no further holonomic constraint needed). It's often still convenient to use non-minimal coordinates anyway: e.g. representing a point on $S^1$ with redundant $(x,y)$ plus the constraint $x^2+y^2=1$, rather than a single angle (avoiding the modulo-$2\pi$ wraparound issue); the same tradeoff motivates using quaternion coordinates $\mathbb{R}^4$ for $SO(3)$ instead of a 3-parameter minimal representation (KIN-01/KIN-02).

## Feedback control, trajectory optimization, path finding: one triad, three roles

DYN-03 and DYN-04 already gave you two of the three standard approaches to getting a robot from $q_\text{start}$ toward a goal:

- **Feedback control** (DYN-03): a *local*, reactive policy, e.g. $q_{t+1}=q_t+J^\sharp(y^*-\phi(q_t))$, correcting error at every step from the current state.
- **Trajectory optimization** (DYN-04): $\operatorname*{argmin}_{q_{0:T}}f(q_{0:T})$, a *local* refinement of an entire trajectory, converging to whatever nearby local optimum the initial guess is closest to.
- **Path finding** (this module and PLAN-02): find *some* $q_{0:T}$ consisting only of valid (feasible) configurations, a fundamentally *global* / discrete search problem, not a local optimization.

The word "local" in the first two is the key limitation both share: neither feedback control nor trajectory optimization has any mechanism for discovering *which side* of an obstacle to go around; both simply follow the local gradient/error signal from wherever they start, and get stuck if that leads into a dead end. Path finding exists precisely to solve this global, topological sub-problem, so that feedback control and trajectory optimization can be handed a starting point (or full initial trajectory) that's already on the right side of every obstacle.

## Classical path-finding heuristics

Before the sampling-based methods of PLAN-02 (the actual state of practice), it's worth seeing why the two obvious classical approaches (gradient-following, grid search) each fall short.

### Potential fields: path finding as gradient descent

Define a **total potential** $U(q):=U_\text{att}(q)+U_\text{rep}(q)$: an attractive term pulling toward the goal (e.g. $U_\text{att}(q)=\tfrac12K_p\|q-q^*\|^2$) plus a repulsive term pushing away from obstacles (growing sharply as $q$ approaches $\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_\text{fea}$). Following the negative gradient, $F(q)=-\nabla U(q)$, as a feedback control law is **exactly gradient descent**, and for the attractive term alone, $F_\text{att}(q)=-\nabla U_\text{att}(q)=K_p(q^*-q)$: **exactly DYN-02's proportional control law**, now reframed as gradient descent on a quadratic potential. This is a genuinely useful reframing: it means "potential field path finding" and "P control toward a goal" are the same computation, and it immediately explains potential fields' central weakness.

<ExerciseCard id="PLAN-01-ex2-potential-field-gradient" />

**The local minimum problem**: because $F(q)=-\nabla U(q)$ only ever follows the *local* downhill direction, the combined potential $U=U_\text{att}+U_\text{rep}$ can have local minima that aren't the goal (e.g. a concave obstacle "cups" the attractive pull into a dead end); gradient descent gets permanently stuck there, with no mechanism to detect or escape it. This is the same local-vs-global limitation named above, now concrete: potential fields *are* feedback control, so they inherit feedback control's blindness to global topology.

### Grid search: wavefront propagation and Dijkstra

An alternative: discretize $\mathcal{X}_\text{fea}$ into a grid, and treat it as a graph search problem. The **wavefront algorithm** starts at the goal cell (value $0$) and repeatedly labels every unlabeled cell adjacent to a labeled cell with (labeled-neighbor-value $+1$), propagating outward like a wavefront until every reachable cell is labeled; following the gradient of these labels (always stepping to a strictly smaller-numbered neighbor) from any start cell produces a shortest grid path, and (unlike potential fields) is provably free of local minima, since a labeled cell's value is exactly its graph-shortest-path distance to the goal. **Dijkstra's algorithm** generalizes this to an arbitrary weighted graph, incrementally labeling nodes with their distance-from-start and provably producing optimal (shortest) paths.

<ExerciseCard id="PLAN-01-ex3-grid-search-scaling" />

Both wavefront and Dijkstra require an actual grid (or graph) over $\mathcal{X}_\text{fea}$ to search: fine in 2D or 3D, but a grid over an $n$-dimensional configuration space has $O(k^n)$ cells for $k$ cells per dimension, the **curse of dimensionality**. A 6-DOF arm at even a coarse $k=20$ per joint already needs $20^6=64$ million cells. This is exactly the gap [PLAN-02](/course/PLAN/PLAN-02)'s sampling-based methods (PRM, RRT) are designed to close: searching $\mathcal{X}_\text{fea}$ via random sampling instead of exhaustive discretization.

## Summary

- **Configuration space** $\mathcal{X}$ is the embedding space for a system's pose description; the **feasible configuration space** $\mathcal{X}_\text{fea}$ (equivalently $C_\text{free}$) is the subset respecting joint limits and non-penetration.
- $d$ independent holonomic constraints reduce $n$ generalized coordinates to $p=n-d$ true **degrees of freedom**; **minimal coordinates** set $n=p$ directly, though non-minimal coordinates are often more convenient in practice.
- **Feedback control** and **trajectory optimization** are both *local* methods; **path finding** is the *global*, discrete search problem that hands them a topologically-correct starting point.
- **Potential fields** are literally gradient descent (their attractive term is exactly DYN-02's P control), and inherit gradient descent's local-minimum problem.
- **Grid search** (wavefront, Dijkstra) is provably free of local minima but suffers the curse of dimensionality, motivating sampling-based methods.
