## Why this matters

This module is where the Math block's tools (gradients, Hessians, Taylor expansion, the covariant gradient) start doing real work: every optimizer used later in this course, from trajectory optimization to training a neural network, is some variation on "take a step informed by the local gradient and curvature." Newton's method, derived properly here, is also the direct ancestor of Gauss-Newton (next module) and the optimal-control formulation your own manipulation-planning research builds on.

## Problem formulation and desiderata

We consider the unconstrained non-linear program $\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^n}f(x)$ for smooth $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, where we can query $f(x)$, $\nabla f(x)$ (gradient methods), and sometimes $\nabla^2f(x)$ (2nd-order methods). We want algorithms that are:

- **monotone**: generate a sequence $x_i$ that gradually reduces $f(x_i)\le f(x_{i-1})$;
- **convergent**: under bounded positive curvature assumptions, we want *exponential* convergence rates, guaranteed;
- **invariant to rescaling of $f$** (multiplying the objective by a constant shouldn't change the path taken);
- **invariant to rescaling of $x$** (or to a linear transform of $x$, i.e. a change of coordinates).

That last one is exactly the contra/covariance theme from [MATH-03](/course/MATH/MATH-03)/[MATH-05](/course/MATH/MATH-05): a genuinely good optimizer shouldn't care whether you measure $x$ in meters or millimeters.

## Gradient descent and the step-size problem

Plain gradient descent iterates $x\leftarrow x-\alpha\nabla f(x)$: cheap (only needs the gradient), but a fixed $\alpha$ is fragile. Near a flat region the gradient is small, so a fixed $\alpha$ takes tiny, wasteful steps; near a steep region the same $\alpha$ can overshoot wildly and even diverge.

## Backtracking line search and the Wolfe conditions

**Line search** in general means solving $\min_{\alpha\ge0}f(x+\alpha\delta)$ for a given step *direction* $\delta$ (separating the two core issues of any downhill method: direction and step size). The most common practical approach is **backtracking**: start with a stepsize guess and shrink it until the step is "good enough."

```
Backtracking Line Search
Input:    initial x, functions f(x) and ∇f(x), tolerance θ,
          parameters (defaults: ϱα⁺=1.2, ϱα⁻=0.5, δmax=∞, ϱls=0.01)
1: initialize stepsize α = 1
2: repeat
3:   δ ← -∇f(x)/‖∇f(x)‖                        // (alternative: δ = -∇f(x))
4:   while f(x+αδ) > f(x) + ϱls·∇f(x)ᵀ(αδ) do    // line search
5:     α ← ϱα⁻·α                                 // REJECT & decrease stepsize
6:   end while
7:   x ← x + αδ                                  // ACCEPT
8:   α ← min{ϱα⁺·α, δmax}                        // increase stepsize
9: until |αδ| < θ                                // perhaps for 10 iterations in sequence
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-optimization.pdf`, p.7)*
>
> Typical defaults: $\varrho_\alpha^+=1.2$, $\varrho_\alpha^-=0.5$, $\varrho_{\text{ls}}=0.01$.

The acceptance test is the **1st Wolfe condition** (the "sufficient decrease condition," also called the **Armijo condition**):

$$f(x+\alpha\delta) \le f(x) + \varrho_{\text{ls}}\nabla f(x)^\top(\alpha\delta)$$

requiring a decrease of $f$ at least $\varrho_{\text{ls}}$-times what the linear model predicted. A stronger, second condition is sometimes added, the **2nd Wolfe condition** (the "curvature condition"):

$$|\nabla f(x+\alpha\delta)^\top\delta| \le \varrho_{\text{ls2}}|\nabla f(x)^\top\delta|, \qquad \varrho_{\text{ls2}}\in(\varrho_{\text{ls}}, \tfrac12)$$

requiring the *slope* along $\delta$ to have decreased by a factor $\varrho_{\text{ls2}}$ too, this rules out steps that are technically decreasing but stop far short of a local minimum along the line (needed for conjugate gradient's convergence guarantee, [OPT-02](/course/OPT/OPT-02)).

> **Theorem (exponential convergence).** Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ have Hessian eigenvalues $\lambda$ bounded by $m<\lambda<M$ (with $m>0$) everywhere. Then gradient descent with backtracking line search converges exponentially, with rate $1-2\tfrac{m}{M}\varrho_{\text{ls}}\varrho_\alpha^-$.

This is a genuinely strong guarantee (proof left to the exercises, per the source, and outside this workbook's scope), but note the rate degrades as $M/m$ (the Hessian's **condition number**) grows: badly-conditioned problems (very different curvature in different directions) converge slowly under plain gradient descent, exactly the motivation for using curvature information directly, next.

## What "steepest descent" actually means

The gradient $-\nabla f(x)$ is often called the "steepest descent direction." Is it, really? A more careful definition: **the steepest descent direction is the one where, taking a step of length 1, you get the largest decrease of $f$ in its linear approximation**:

$$\delta^* = \operatorname*{argmin}_\delta \nabla f(x)^\top\delta \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \|\delta\|=1$$

But "length 1" depends on a choice of metric: $\|\delta\|^2=\delta^\top A\delta$ for some SPD matrix $A$ (the Euclidean case is just $A=I$). This is exactly [MATH-05](/course/MATH/MATH-05)'s covariant-gradient setup, and the source derives it slightly differently, worth seeing both routes:

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-01-ex1c-steepest-descent-cholesky" />

**Behavior under linear coordinate transformations** (recapping [MATH-03](/course/MATH/MATH-03)'s contra/covariance table): if $z=Bx$ is a change of coordinates, the gradient transforms as $\nabla_zf(z)=B^{-\top}\nabla_xf(x)$, the metric transforms as $A_z=B^{-\top}A_xB^{-1}$, and the steepest descent direction $A_z^{-1}\nabla_zf(z) = BA_x^{-1}\nabla_xf(x)$ transforms exactly like an ordinary coordinate vector, i.e. it is **covariant**. This is precisely why $-\nabla f(x)$ alone (implicitly $A=I$) is *not* invariant to rescaling $x$, violating the desiderata above, and why Newton's method (next) fixes this.

## Newton's method, from root-finding up

Newton's method is usually introduced for finding roots (zero points) of a function $g$:

$$x \leftarrow x - \frac{g(x)}{g'(x)}$$

(approximate $g$ by its tangent line at $x$, and jump to where that tangent line crosses zero). To find optima of $f(x)$ in 1D, apply this to $g=f'$ (since optima are roots of the derivative):

$$x \leftarrow x - \frac{f'(x)}{f''(x)}$$

And in higher dimensions, $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\boxed{x \leftarrow x - \nabla^2f(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x)}$$

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-01-ex2-newton-derivation" />

**Stop and try**: check that Newton's step reduces to something sensible in 1D: for $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $\delta^* = -f'(x)/f''(x)$. Confirm this matches $-H(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x)$ in the $n=1$ case.

**Notes on the Newton step** (several genuinely important, easy-to-miss properties):

- If $f$ is a 2nd-order polynomial (truly quadratic), the Newton step jumps to the optimum in exactly **one step**.
- Unlike the gradient magnitude $|\nabla f(x)|$, the Newton step's magnitude $|\delta|$ is **meaningful and scale-invariant**: rescaling $f$ (e.g. trading cents for euros) leaves $\delta$ unchanged, since $\nabla^2f$ and $\nabla f$ rescale by the same factor and cancel.
- Unlike the gradient $\nabla f(x)$, the Newton step $\delta$ is a **true vector**: it's invariant under linear coordinate transformations, its coordinates transform contra-variantly, exactly as they should for a genuine vector (not merely a "coordinate-valued 1-form" the way a raw gradient is). The proof is identical to the steepest-descent-under-a-metric argument above, with the Hessian playing the role of the metric $A$.

## Worked example

Let $f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top A x - b^\top x$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$, $b=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}$, starting at $x_0=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}$. Since $\nabla f(x)=Ax-b$ and $H=A$ (constant, since $f$ is quadratic):

$$\nabla f(x_0) = A(0)-b = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \delta^* = -A^{-1}\nabla f(x_0)$$

Solving $A\delta^*=-\nabla f(x_0)$ (rather than explicitly inverting $A$) gives $\delta^* \approx \begin{pmatrix}0.4545\\-0.8182\end{pmatrix}$, so $x_1 = x_0+\delta^* \approx \begin{pmatrix}0.4545\\-0.8182\end{pmatrix}$.

*(Verified with NumPy: $x_1$ matches $A^{-1}b$, the exact minimizer, exactly, since a single Newton step on a genuinely quadratic $f$ always lands exactly on the minimizer, not just approximately closer to it.)*

## When the quadratic model isn't exact: line search

For a non-quadratic $f$, the quadratic model is only locally accurate, so a full Newton step $\delta^*$ might overshoot. Backtracking line search (above) fixes this by trying $\alpha=1$ first, then shrinking:

```
Basic Newton Method
Input:    initial x, functions f(x), ∇f(x), ∇²f(x), tolerance θ,
          parameters (defaults: ϱα⁺=1.2, ϱα⁻=0.5, ϱls=0.01, λ)
1: initialize stepsize α = 1, fixed damping λ
2: repeat
3:   compute δ to solve (∇²f(x) + λI)δ = -∇f(x)
4:   while f(x+αδ) > f(x) + ϱls·∇f(x)ᵀ(αδ) do    // line search
5:     α ← ϱα⁻·α                                 // decrease stepsize
6:   end while
7:   x ← x + αδ                                  // step is accepted
8:   α ← min{ϱα⁺·α, 1}                            // increase stepsize
9: until |αδ|∞ < θ
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-optimization.pdf`, p.12. Line 3 computes the Newton step δ = -∇²f(x)⁻¹∇f(x), e.g. using a Cholesky-based linear solve.)*

## When the Hessian isn't positive definite

Newton's method as derived assumes $\nabla^2f(x)$ is invertible and positive definite, i.e. the quadratic model has a genuine minimum. Two things can go wrong:

- **If the Hessian is negative definite**, the Newton step jumps to a **maximum** instead (it's still the exact stationary point of the local quadratic model, just the wrong kind).
- **If some eigenvalues are positive and some negative** (a **saddle point**, MATH-02's classification), the "Newton step" isn't well-defined as a sensible descent direction at all.

**Fix**: a robust Newton method checks whether the proposed step actually points downhill ($\nabla f(x)^\top\delta>0$ after a sign flip, or the linear solve fails outright because $\nabla^2f(x)+\lambda I$ isn't positive definite), and if not, falls back to the plain gradient direction $\delta\leftarrow-\nabla f(x)/\|\nabla f(x)\|$ for that step. The damping constant $\lambda$ can also be increased (shifting all the Hessian's eigenvalues up by $\lambda$, [MATH-04](/course/MATH/MATH-04)'s shift-invariance fact) until $\nabla^2f(x)+\lambda I$ becomes positive definite. This combination is called **Levenberg-Marquardt damping**, and is closely related to **trust-region methods**: the damped Newton step is exactly the solution to $\min_\delta\left[\nabla f(x)^\top\delta+\tfrac12\delta^\top\nabla^2f(x)\delta+\tfrac12\lambda\delta^2\right]$, a squared penalty on step size, while a trust-region method solves the same objective subject to an explicit hard constraint $\delta^2\le\beta$; both converge to $-\nabla f(x)$ as the damping/trust-region shrinks to force a tiny step, and to the plain Newton step as it's relaxed.

## Summary

- A good downhill optimizer should be monotone, (exponentially) convergent, and invariant to rescaling of both $f$ and $x$.
- Backtracking line search accepts a step once the 1st Wolfe/Armijo condition holds; the 2nd Wolfe (curvature) condition is a stronger variant needed by conjugate gradient. Gradient descent with backtracking converges exponentially at a rate governed by the Hessian's condition number $M/m$.
- The steepest-descent direction under a general metric $A$ is $-A^{-1}\nabla f(x)$, derived via a Cholesky substitution; this transforms covariantly under coordinate changes, unlike the plain gradient.
- Newton's method, $\delta^*=-\nabla^2f(x)^{-1}\nabla f(x)$, is derived first as root-finding on $\nabla f$, then recognized as steepest descent under the Hessian-as-metric. It's scale-invariant and coordinate-invariant, unlike plain gradient descent, and jumps to the exact optimum in one step for quadratic $f$.
- A non-positive-definite Hessian breaks Newton's method (jumps to a max or an ill-defined saddle direction); the fix is a gradient-direction fallback and/or Levenberg-Marquardt damping, closely related to trust-region methods.
