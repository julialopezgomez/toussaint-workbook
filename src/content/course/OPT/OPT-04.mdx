## Why this matters

Convexity is the property that separates "optimization problems you can actually solve reliably" from "optimization problems where you're at the mercy of your starting point." Recognizing when a subproblem is convex (or can be made convex, e.g. via a QP approximation) is a constant judgment call in trajectory optimization and motion planning, exactly the kind of problem your own manipulation-planning research deals with. This module also covers the three practical workhorses for actually solving a constrained problem: log barrier, augmented Lagrangian, and SQP.

## Convexity, quasiconvexity, unimodality

> A set $X$ is **convex** iff $\forall x,y\in X, a\in[0,1]: ax+(1-a)y\in X$. A function is **convex** iff $\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n,a\in[0,1]: f(ax+(1-a)y)\le af(x)+(1-a)f(y)$. A function is **quasiconvex** iff $f(ax+(1-a)y)\le\max\{f(x),f(y)\}$ (equivalently: every sublevel set $\{x:f(x)\le\alpha\}$ is convex). A function is **unimodal** iff it has exactly one local minimum, which is therefore the global one.
>
> These form a strict hierarchy: $\text{convex}\subset\text{quasiconvex}\subset\text{unimodal}\subset\text{general}$.

Properties worth having on hand: the sum of two convex functions is convex; $f\in\mathcal{C}^2$ is convex $\iff$ $\nabla^2f(x)$ is PSD everywhere (the Hessian-eigenvalue connection from [MATH-02](/course/MATH/MATH-02)); a convex $f$'s sublevel sets are convex; and the dual function $l(\lambda)=\min_xL(x,\lambda)$ is *always* concave (a pointwise minimization of functions linear in $\lambda$), which is exactly why [OPT-03](/course/OPT/OPT-03)'s dual problem is always convex regardless of the primal.

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-04-ex1-why-convexity-matters" />

This is the single biggest reason convex problems (including LPs and QPs, below) are so valuable: any local optimization method (gradient descent, Newton) that finds *a* local minimum has automatically found *the* global minimum, no need to worry about getting stuck.

## Linear and quadratic programs

A **linear program (LP)**: $\min_x c^\top x$ s.t. $Gx\le h, Ax=b$ (standard form: $\min_xc^\top x$ s.t. $x\ge0,Ax=b$). A **quadratic program (QP)**: $\min_x\tfrac12x^\top Qx+c^\top x$ s.t. $Gx\le h,Ax=b$, with $Q$ positive definite. Both are convex whenever correctly set up, so local solutions are global.

Converting a general LP to standard form uses **slack variables**: introduce $\xi\ge0$ so $Gx\le h$ becomes the equality $Gx+\xi=h$, and split $x=x^+-x^-$ with $x^+,x^-\ge0$ (since standard form needs $x\ge0$, but the original $x$ may be any sign). A slack variable is exactly "a variable added to an inequality to turn it into an equality plus a non-negativity constraint."

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-04-ex3-lp-recognition" />

**Example QP: Support Vector Machines.** The (primal) SVM problem $\min_{\beta,\xi}\|\beta\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i$ s.t. $y_i(x_i^\top\beta)\ge1-\xi_i,\xi_i\ge0$ is a QP; its Lagrangian dual, $\max_\alpha-\tfrac14\sum_{i,i'}\alpha_i\alpha_{i'}y_iy_{i'}x_i^\top x_{i'}+\sum_i\alpha_i$ s.t. $0\le\alpha_i\le C$, is *also* convex (as [OPT-03](/course/OPT/OPT-03) guarantees generally), and only ever needs $x_i^\top x_{i'}$ (inner products), the basis for kernelization. Data points with $\alpha_i>0$ are exactly the **support vectors** (by complementary slackness: $\alpha_i>0$ forces the corresponding margin constraint to be tight).

The **Simplex algorithm** (Dantzig, 1947) solves LPs by walking along the edges of the constraint **polytope**: since an LP's optimum always sits at a vertex, at each vertex it moves to the adjacent edge that decreases $c^\top x$ the most, until no edge improves further. It's often efficient in practice but exponential in the worst case; **interior point methods** (log barrier, below) instead approach the optimum through the polytope's interior, with polynomial worst-case guarantees.

## The log-barrier method

One practical way to solve a constrained problem: replace each inequality constraint with a penalty term that blows up near the boundary, turning the constrained problem into a sequence of unconstrained ones:

$$\min_xB(x,\mu), \qquad B(x,\mu) = f(x) - \mu\sum_i\log(-g_i(x))$$

As $\mu\to0$, the barrier $-\mu\log(-g)$ converges to $\infty\cdot[g>0]$ (an exact indicator-style barrier), but starting with tiny $\mu$ makes $B$ non-smooth from the start, so $\mu$ is **gradually decreased**. (You'll sometimes see this written with $t=1/\mu$ instead, weight *growing* rather than shrinking, an equivalent, interchangeable convention, e.g. in the exercise below.)

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-04-ex2-log-barrier-derivation" />

```
Log Barrier Method
Input:    initial x, functions f(x), g(x), tolerance θ,
          parameters (defaults: ϱμ⁻=0.5, μ0=1)
1: initialize μ = μ0
2: repeat
3:   centering: solve unconstrained problem x ← argmin_x B(x,μ)
4:   decrease μ ← ϱμ⁻·μ
5: until |Δx| < θ                                // repeatedly
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-optimization.pdf`, p.25. Centering is typically solved via Newton, warm-started from the previous x.)*

Varying $\mu$ traces out the **central path**, points $x^*(\mu)$ gradually approaching the true optimum from the *interior* as $\mu\to0$ (this is exactly why it's an **interior point method**: every iterate strictly satisfies $g_i(x)<0$). Remarkably, **centering is equivalent to solving a relaxed KKT system**: at $x^*(\mu)$, $\nabla f(x)-\sum_i\frac{\mu}{g_i(x)}\nabla g_i(x)=0$, which matches KKT stationarity exactly if you define $\lambda_i=-\mu/g_i(x)$ (automatically $\ge0$ in the interior) — but complementarity becomes $\lambda_ig_i(x)=-\mu$ instead of exactly $0$, a **relaxed complementarity** condition. So $\mu$ has a dual meaning: it's both the barrier strength *and* an upper bound on the KKT sub-optimality at each centering step, $f(x^*(\mu))-p^*\le m\mu$ (with $m$ the number of inequality constraints).

## Squared penalty and the augmented Lagrangian

A simpler (but cruder) alternative: a **squared penalty**, zero when feasible, growing quadratically when violated: $S(x,\mu,\nu)=f(x)+\mu\sum_i[g_i(x)>0]g_i(x)^2+\nu\sum_jh_j(x)^2$. Simple and gives good Newton conditioning, but always leaves *some* constraint violation (exact feasibility only as $\mu,\nu\to\infty$, which itself causes ill-conditioning).

```
Squared Penalty Method
Input:    initial x, functions f(x), g(x), h(x), tolerances θ, ε,
          parameters (defaults: ϱμ⁺=ϱν⁺=10, μ0=ν0=1)
1: initialize μ = μ0, ν = ν0
2: repeat
3:   solve unconstrained problem x ← argmin_x S(x,μ,ν)
4:   μ ← ϱμ⁺·μ,  ν ← ϱν⁺·ν
5: until |Δx| ≤ θ  and  ∀i,j: gᵢ(x) ≤ ε, |hⱼ(x)| ≤ ε   // repeatedly
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-optimization.pdf`, p.27. μ and ν are increased gradually; quadratic penalties give good conditioning for Newton methods, but always leave some violation.)*

The **augmented Lagrangian** fixes this without sending the penalty weight to infinity, by additionally tracking multiplier estimates $\kappa_j$ (self-contained derivation, equality constraints first): minimize $A(x,\kappa,\nu)=f(x)+\sum_j\kappa_jh_j(x)+\nu\sum_jh_j(x)^2$, then **update** $\kappa_j$ using the gradient at the new minimizer:

$$\kappa_j^{\text{new}} = \kappa_j^{\text{old}} + 2\nu h_j(x')$$

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-04-ex5-auglag-update" />

```
Augmented Lagrangian
Input:    initial x, functions f(x), g(x), h(x), tolerances θ, ε,
          parameters (defaults: ϱμ⁺=ϱν⁺=1.2, μ0=ν0=1)
1: initialize μ=μ0, ν=ν0, λᵢ=0, κⱼ=0
2: repeat
3:   solve unconstrained problem x ← argmin_x A(x,λ,κ,μ,ν)
4:   ∀i: λᵢ ← max(λᵢ + 2μ·gᵢ(x'), 0),  ∀j: κⱼ ← κⱼ + 2ν·hⱼ(x')
5:   optionally: μ ← ϱμ⁺·μ, ν ← ϱν⁺·ν
6: until |Δx| < θ  and  gᵢ(x) < ε  and  |hⱼ(x)| < ε        // repeatedly
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-optimization.pdf`, p.30. Unlike log-barrier/squared-penalty, this does not require μ,ν→∞ for exact constraint satisfaction.)*

Why this is elegant: it does **not** require the penalty limit $\nu\to\infty$ to achieve exact constraint satisfaction, avoiding the ill-conditioning that plagues both the log barrier (as $\mu\to0$) and squared penalty (as $\nu\to\infty$) methods. If $f,h$ were linear, the very next iteration's $\kappa_j$ would be *exactly* right, hitting the constraint exactly, by construction. For inequalities, the same idea applies with $\lambda_i\leftarrow\max(\lambda_i+2\mu g_i(x'),0)$ (clipped at zero, since $\lambda_i\ge0$ is required), toggling between "active, penalize" and "inactive, ignore" as needed each iteration.

## SQP: Newton's method for the constrained case

**Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP)** generalizes Newton's method to the constrained case directly, without an unconstrained-inner-loop reformulation: in each iteration, Taylor-expand the cost to 2nd order and the constraints to 1st order around the current $x$, then solve the resulting QP exactly for the step $\delta$:

$$\min_\delta f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top\delta+\tfrac12\delta^\top\nabla^2f(x)\delta \quad\text{s.t.}\quad g(x)+\nabla g(x)^\top\delta\le0,\ h(x)+\nabla h(x)^\top\delta=0$$

If $f$ were exactly quadratic and $g,h$ exactly linear, $\delta^*$ would jump directly to the true optimum in one step (same as plain Newton on an unconstrained quadratic). Each SQP iteration requires a full constrained QP solve (which may itself need an inner log-barrier or augmented-Lagrangian loop, or a dedicated QP solver), but the **outer** loop never re-queries the original nonlinear $f,g,h$ more than once per iteration, an attractive query-efficiency trade whenever evaluating $f,g,h$ is the expensive part.

## Summary

- Convex $\subset$ quasiconvex $\subset$ unimodal $\subset$ general; a local minimum of a convex function is automatically global, this is what makes convex problems tractable, and $\nabla^2f\succeq0$ everywhere is the $\mathcal{C}^2$ criterion.
- LPs/QPs are convex when correctly formulated; standard form uses slack variables; the SVM dual is a canonical QP example; Simplex walks the polytope's edges, log barrier approaches from the interior.
- The log-barrier method centers a sequence of unconstrained problems along the central path as $\mu\to0$; centering is exactly a relaxed-complementarity KKT system, with $\mu$ an upper bound on sub-optimality.
- The augmented Lagrangian avoids the ill-conditioning of penalty/barrier methods as their weight $\to\infty$, by explicitly updating multiplier estimates $\kappa_j\leftarrow\kappa_j+2\nu h_j(x')$ instead.
- SQP generalizes Newton's method directly to the constrained case: Taylor-expand cost (2nd order) and constraints (1st order), solve the resulting QP exactly, repeat.
