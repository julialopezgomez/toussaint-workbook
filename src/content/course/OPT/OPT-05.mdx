## Why this matters

Every neural network you'll ever train (including the manual-MLP-to-PyTorch bridge in ML-03) uses SGD or one of its refinements, not full-batch gradient descent or Newton's method, precisely because real datasets are too large for exact per-step gradients to be affordable. Separately, the No Free Lunch theorem is worth understanding properly once, since it's frequently misquoted to mean something far more nihilistic than what it actually says.

## The full-batch vs. mini-batch tradeoff

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-05-ex1-why-sgd" />

## Worked example

Let $f_i(x) = \tfrac12(x-a_i)^2$ over dataset $a=[1,3,5,2,4]$, so the full-batch gradient at $x_0=0$ is $\tfrac15\sum_i(0-a_i) = \tfrac15(-15) = -3$.

Using a mini-batch $\{a_1,a_4\}=\{1,2\}$ instead: $\widehat{\nabla f}(0) = \tfrac12[(0-1)+(0-2)] = -1.5$, noticeably different from the true $-3$. With $\alpha=0.1$: $x_1 = 0 - 0.1(-1.5) = 0.15$, versus $x_1=0.3$ if the full-batch gradient had been used instead.

*(Both gradients verified with NumPy while authoring this module.)* Neither mini-batch estimate is "wrong", each is an unbiased sample of the true gradient, just one particular noisy realization of it. Averaged over many random mini-batches, the estimate converges to the true gradient.

## Momentum and Adam, briefly

Two widely-used refinements reduce the practical impact of gradient noise:

- **Momentum** accumulates a running average of recent gradients (rather than using only the current step's noisy estimate), smoothing out the noise and helping push through shallow regions consistently pointing the same general direction.
- **Adam** additionally adapts a per-parameter step size based on a running estimate of each parameter's gradient variance, taking smaller steps for noisy/high-variance parameters and larger steps for consistently-informative ones.

Both are covered here conceptually; you'll use them directly (via PyTorch's built-in optimizers) rather than re-deriving them from scratch, when you get to training networks in later modules.

## The No Free Lunch theorem

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-05-ex3-nfl-conceptual" />

## Summary

- SGD trades exact per-step gradients for many more, cheaper, noisy-but-unbiased steps, a good trade whenever the dataset is large.
- Momentum smooths gradient noise via a running average; Adam additionally adapts per-parameter step sizes.
- The No Free Lunch theorem is about averaging over ALL possible objective functions (including ones with no real-world analogue), not a statement that optimization method choice doesn't matter on the structured problems you'll actually encounter.
