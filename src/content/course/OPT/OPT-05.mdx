## Why this matters

Every neural network you'll ever train (including the manual-MLP-to-PyTorch bridge in ML-03) uses SGD or one of its refinements, not full-batch gradient descent or Newton's method, precisely because real datasets are too large for exact per-step gradients to be affordable. Separately, the No Free Lunch theorem is worth understanding properly once, since it's frequently misquoted to mean something far more nihilistic than what it actually says.

## The full-batch vs. mini-batch tradeoff

For an **additive cost function**, $\min_wf(w)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nf_i(w)$ (e.g. a per-data-point loss plus regularizer, $f(w)=\frac1n\sum_i\ell(f(x_i;w),y_i)+\frac\lambda2\|w\|^2$), computing $\nabla f$ exactly requires touching every one of the (possibly huge) $n$ data points, every single step.

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-05-ex1-why-sgd" />

**Stochastic gradient descent** instead computes only $\nabla f_i(w)$ for one (or a mini-batch of) randomly-sampled component(s):

```
Stochastic Gradient Descent
Input:    initial w0 ∈ ℝⁿ, gradient functions ∇fᵢ(w), stepsize schedule αk
1: for k = 0, 1, 2, ... do
2:   sample i uniformly (iid) from {1,..,n}
3:   wk+1 ← wk - αk·∇fᵢ(wk)
4: end for
```
```
Stochastic Gradient Descent (episodic)
Input:    initial w0 ∈ ℝⁿ, gradient functions ∇fᵢ(w), stepsize schedule αk
1: initialize k = 0
2: for episode j = 0, 1, ... do
3:   for i = 1, .., n (or i = RandomPermutation({1,..,n})) do
4:     wk+1 ← wk - αk·∇fᵢ(wk)
5:     k ← k + 1
6:   end for
7: end for
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-optimization.pdf`, p.57. $\nabla f_i(w)$ has expectation $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla f_i(w)\}=\nabla f(w)$.)*

Crucially, $\nabla f_i(w)$ is an **unbiased** estimator: $\mathbb{E}\{\nabla f_i(w)\}=\nabla f(w)$, so on average across many steps, SGD behaves like gradient descent, just noisier per step, in exchange for being $n$ times cheaper per step.

## Worked example

Let $f_i(x) = \tfrac12(x-a_i)^2$ over dataset $a=[1,3,5,2,4]$, so the full-batch gradient at $x_0=0$ is $\tfrac15\sum_i(0-a_i) = \tfrac15(-15) = -3$.

Using a mini-batch $\{a_1,a_4\}=\{1,2\}$ instead: $\widehat{\nabla f}(0) = \tfrac12[(0-1)+(0-2)] = -1.5$, noticeably different from the true $-3$. With $\alpha=0.1$: $x_1 = 0 - 0.1(-1.5) = 0.15$, versus $x_1=0.3$ if the full-batch gradient had been used instead.

*(Both gradients verified with NumPy while authoring this module.)* Neither mini-batch estimate is "wrong", each is an unbiased sample of the true gradient, just one particular noisy realization of it. Averaged over many random mini-batches, the estimate converges to the true gradient.

## Convergence of SGD

SGD is a method to find a point where $\nabla f(w)\approx0$; convergence analysis studies how $\|\nabla f(w_k)\|$ decreases with $k$, in expectation. The standard assumption:

> **Definition (Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla f$).** $\exists L\in\mathbb{R}$ s.t. $\forall w,\bar w: \|\nabla f(w)-\nabla f(\bar w)\|\le L\|w-\bar w\|$ ("the change of gradient $\nabla f(w)$ is limited").

> **Theorem.** Assuming $\nabla f$ is $L$-continuous and $\text{Var}\{\nabla f_i(w)\}=\sigma^2$, then
> $$\min_k\{\mathbb{E}\{\|\nabla f(w_k)\|^2\}\} \le \frac{f(w_0)-f^*}{\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\alpha_k} + \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\alpha_k^2}{\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\alpha_k}\cdot\frac{L\sigma^2}{2}$$

The derivation is a genuinely instructive chain of first-order Taylor bounds, worth walking through once (not for its own sake, but because the same "Lipschitz-bound-then-telescope" pattern recurs across ML/optimization theory):

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-05-ex2b-convergence-derivation" />

**Implications**: with a constant step size $\alpha$ and no gradient noise ($\sigma=0$, i.e. plain gradient descent), this recovers $O(1/t)$ convergence. With genuine stochasticity, a **constant** $\alpha$ is a bad choice, the bound's second term becomes a non-vanishing constant $\alpha L\sigma^2/2$: SGD's gradient-norm bound never shrinks to zero, it plateaus at a noise floor. A **diminishing** step size $\alpha_k=\alpha_0/(1+\gamma k)$ is the standard fix, giving $\sum_k\alpha_k=O(\log t)$ and error $O(1/\log t)$, slower than plain GD's $O(1/t)$, but SGD's steps are $n\times$ cheaper, and for large $n$ this trade is decisively worth it (the source's tabulated comparison: full-batch needs $O(\log\frac1\rho)$ iterations at cost $n$ each to reach precision $\rho$, while SGD needs more iterations but at cost $1$ each, and for large $n$, SGD reaches a target *excess error* $\varepsilon$ in less total time).

## Improving on basic SGD: variance reduction, adaptive scaling, momentum

Three practical directions improve on plain SGD, worth knowing by name since you'll meet all of them as PyTorch optimizer options:

**Adaptive per-coordinate scaling** (estimate the diagonal of the Hessian, or "scale" the gradient coordinate-wise, since a full stochastic BFGS is complex):

$$\text{RMSprop: } v_k\leftarrow(1-\eta)v_{k-1}+\eta[\nabla f_i(w_k)]^2, \quad w_{k+1}\leftarrow w_k-\frac{\alpha_k}{\sqrt{v_k+\mu}}\nabla f_i(w_k) \quad\text{(elementwise)}$$

$$\text{Adagrad: } v_k\leftarrow v_{k-1}+[\nabla f_i(w_k)]^2, \quad w_{k+1}\leftarrow w_k-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{v_k+\mu}}\nabla f_i(w_k) \quad\text{(elementwise)}$$

Dividing by $\sqrt{\langle g^2\rangle}$ (a running average of squared gradients) makes the update **scale-invariant** (rescaling $f_i\to af_i$ scales both $\nabla f_i$ and $\sqrt{\langle g^2\rangle}$ equally, cancelling), accounts for different conditioning along different coordinates, and, since $\sqrt{\langle g^2\rangle}$ is roughly proportional to curvature for a quadratic $f_i$, mimics a cheap diagonal-Hessian preconditioner.

**Momentum** (cf. conjugate gradient, [OPT-02](/course/OPT/OPT-02)): $w_{k+1}\leftarrow w_k-\alpha_k\nabla f_i(w_k)+\beta_k(w_k-w_{k-1})$, a low-pass filter on the step direction that smooths gradient noise. **Nesterov's** variant evaluates the gradient at a *look-ahead* point $\tilde w_k=w_k+\beta_k(w_k-w_{k-1})$ instead of $w_k$ itself, giving $O(1/k^2)$ convergence for convex problems (vs. plain gradient descent's $O(1/k)$).

**Adam** combines momentum-style averaging of the gradient ($m_t$) *and* RMSprop-style averaging of the squared gradient ($v_t$), with bias correction (since both start at zero, biased low early on):

```
Adam
Require:  α: Stepsize (default 0.001)
Require:  β1, β2 ∈ [0,1): exponential decay rates for the moment estimates
          (defaults β1=0.9, β2=0.999, ε=10⁻⁸)
Require:  f(θ): stochastic objective function
Require:  θ0: initial parameter vector
1: m0 ← 0                          (initialize 1st moment vector)
2: v0 ← 0                          (initialize 2nd moment vector)
3: t ← 0                           (initialize timestep)
4: while θt not converged do
5:   t ← t + 1
6:   gt ← ∇θft(θt-1)                          (get gradient at timestep t)
7:   mt ← β1·mt-1 + (1-β1)·gt                  (update biased 1st moment estimate)
8:   vt ← β2·vt-1 + (1-β2)·gt²                 (update biased 2nd raw moment estimate; elementwise)
9:   m̂t ← mt / (1-β1ᵗ)                         (bias-corrected 1st moment estimate)
10:  v̂t ← vt / (1-β2ᵗ)                         (bias-corrected 2nd raw moment estimate)
11:  θt ← θt-1 - α·m̂t/(√v̂t + ε)                (update parameters; elementwise)
12: end while
13: return θt
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-optimization.pdf`, p.62, reproducing Algorithm 1 of Kingma & Ba, "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization," arXiv:1412.6980. gₜ² indicates the elementwise square gₜ⊙gₜ.)*

## The No Free Lunch theorem

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-05-ex3-nfl-conceptual" />

Formally: assume a finite space $X$, a distribution $P(f)$ over functions $f:X\to Y$, and a non-revisiting algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ generating queries and observing outcomes.

> **Theorem (No Free Lunch).** $\exists h:Y\to\mathbb{R}$ s.t. $\forall K, \{x_1,..,x_K\}\subset X: P(f_{x_1},..,f_{x_K})=\prod_{k=1}^Kh(f_{x_k})$ $\iff$ $\forall\mathcal{A},\forall T: P(y_{1:T};\mathcal{A}) = \prod_{i=1}^Th(y_i)$ (independent of $\mathcal{A}$).

In words: **$P(f)$ factorizes (no structure/correlation across different inputs' function values) $\iff$ all algorithms generate the same random observations**, i.e. no algorithm can do better than any other *on average over such a fully unstructured prior*. This is a precise, narrow statement about averaging over **all possible functions with no assumed structure whatsoever** (including wildly discontinuous, adversarial ones with zero real-world analogue), not a claim that every algorithm performs equally on the *structured* problems (smooth, Lipschitz, sparse-Hessian, convex, etc.) you'll actually encounter, where assuming structure (as every method in this block does) is exactly what buys you an advantage.

## Summary

- SGD trades exact per-step gradients for many more, cheaper, noisy-but-unbiased steps; the convergence bound (derived from Lipschitz continuity via a telescoping sum) shows constant step sizes plateau at a noise floor, while diminishing step sizes $\alpha_k=\alpha_0/(1+\gamma k)$ still converge, just more slowly than full-batch GD.
- RMSprop/Adagrad adaptively rescale each coordinate by a running estimate of its squared gradient, approximating a diagonal-Hessian preconditioner; momentum/Nesterov smooth gradient noise via a running average of the step direction itself.
- Adam combines both ideas (momentum on the gradient, RMSprop-style scaling) with bias correction for the early iterations.
- The No Free Lunch theorem is about averaging over ALL possible objective functions with a fully factorized (structureless) prior, not a statement that optimizer choice doesn't matter on the structured problems you'll actually encounter.
