## Why this matters

Almost every real robotics optimization problem has constraints: joint limits, collision avoidance, dynamics feasibility, contact conditions. The Lagrangian and KKT conditions are the foundation every constrained solver (interior point, SQP, augmented Lagrangian, all covered in [OPT-04](/course/OPT/OPT-04)) builds on. Getting comfortable solving small constrained problems by hand here makes those later methods feel like variations on one idea, rather than a new toolbox each time.

## Problem formulation

The general (constrained) **Non-linear Mathematical Program (NLP)**: for $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, $g:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, $h:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^l$,

$$\min_x f(x) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad g(x)\le0,\ h(x)=0$$

We typically assume $f,g,h$ differentiable, and can query $f,\nabla f,g,\nabla g,h,\nabla h$ (and optionally $\nabla^2f$). By convention: costs $f(x)$ **pull**, constraints $g(x),h(x)$ **push back**.

## Why $\nabla f(x)=0$ isn't the right condition anymore

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-03-ex1-why-not-just-zero-gradient" />

At the optimum, there must be a **balance** between the cost gradient $-\nabla f(x)$ pulling, and the gradients of the *active* constraints $-\nabla g_i(x)$ pushing back (inactive constraints, not touching their boundary, contribute nothing).

## The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions

> **Theorem (Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions).** For any NLP, $x$ optimal $\Rightarrow$ there exist $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^m,\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^l$ such that
> $$\nabla f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i\nabla g_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^l\kappa_j\nabla h_j(x) = 0 \qquad \text{(stationarity)}$$
> $$\forall j:\ h_j(x)=0, \qquad \forall i:\ g_i(x)\le0 \qquad \text{(primal feasibility)}$$
> $$\forall i:\ \lambda_i\ge0 \qquad \text{(dual feasibility)}$$
> $$\forall i:\ \lambda_ig_i(x)=0 \qquad \text{(complementarity)}$$

Four conditions worth having named separately, each with its own job:

- **Stationarity** is the "force balance": existence of $\lambda,\kappa$ making this hold is equivalent to $\nabla f(x)\in\text{span}\{\nabla g_{1..m}(x),\nabla h_{1..l}(x)\}$, i.e. the cost gradient has *no component* left over that the constraint gradients can't cancel. The values of $\lambda,\kappa$ tell you how strongly each constraint is pushing.
- **Complementarity** ("the logic of constraint activity") elegantly packages two cases into one equation: either the constraint is **inactive** ($g_i(x)<0$) and its multiplier is zero ($\lambda_i=0$, it contributes nothing), or it's **active** ($g_i(x)=0$, touching its boundary) and can have $\lambda_i>0$. The combinatorics of *which* constraints end up active ($O(2^m)$ possibilities) is a core source of difficulty in constrained optimization; if you already knew which constraints were active, the problem would reduce to an easier equality-constrained one.

## The Lagrangian

The Lagrangian packages the objective and all constraints into a single function:

$$L(x,\lambda,\kappa) = f(x) + \lambda^\top g(x) + \kappa^\top h(x) = f(x) + \sum_i\lambda_ig_i(x)+\sum_j\kappa_jh_j(x)$$

where $\lambda_i\ge0,\kappa_j\in\mathbb{R}$ are called **dual variables** or Lagrange multipliers. Why define this? Three reasons: it relates directly to the KKT conditions; it's useful for computing optima analytically, on paper; and optima are necessarily **saddle points** of the Lagrangian, which motivates a **dual problem**, sometimes easier to solve than the original (**primal**) one.

**Relations between the Lagrangian and KKT**: $\nabla_xL=0$ *implies* the 1st KKT condition (stationarity) directly; $\nabla_\kappa L=0$ implies primal feasibility for the equalities; and maximizing over $\lambda\ge0$, $\max_{\lambda\ge0}L(x,\lambda)$, is related to the remaining (2nd and 4th) KKT conditions for the inequalities (worked through in the exercise below). In short: **$\min_xL(x,\lambda,\kappa)$ reproduces the 1st KKT condition; $\max_{\lambda\ge0,\kappa}L(x,\lambda,\kappa)$ reproduces the rest.**

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-03-ex2-lagrangian-derivation" />

**Stop and try**: before continuing, note what makes this trick work: $\nabla_\kappa L(x,\kappa) = h(x)$, so setting *that* to zero recovers the constraint itself. The Lagrangian's full stationarity conditions ($\nabla_x L=0$ and $\nabla_\kappa L=0$ together) exactly recover both the parallel-gradients condition and the original constraint, as a single unconstrained problem in $(x,\kappa)$.

## Worked example

Minimize $f(x)=\tfrac12(x_1^2+x_2^2)$ subject to $x_1-x_2=1$. Lagrangian: $L(x,\kappa) = \tfrac12(x_1^2+x_2^2)+\kappa(x_1-x_2-1)$.

$$\nabla_x L = \begin{pmatrix}x_1+\kappa\\x_2-\kappa\end{pmatrix}=0 \implies x_1=-\kappa,\ x_2=\kappa$$

Substituting into the constraint: $-\kappa-\kappa=1 \implies \kappa=-0.5$, giving $x_1^*=0.5$, $x_2^*=-0.5$.

*(Check: $0.5-(-0.5)=1$ ✓. Verified with a direct linear solve of the full KKT system while authoring this module.)*

## Primal, dual, and duality

Restricting to inequalities for simplicity, define $F_\infty(x)=\max_{\lambda\ge0}L(x,\lambda)$ (this equals $f(x)$ when $g(x)\le0$, and $+\infty$ otherwise, an "infinity-barrier" reformulation of the constraint) and the **dual function** $l(\lambda)=\min_xL(x,\lambda)$. This defines two nested optimization problems:

$$\min_x\underbrace{\max_{\lambda\ge0}L(x,\lambda)}_{F_\infty(x)} \qquad\text{(primal problem)} \qquad\qquad \max_{\lambda\ge0}\underbrace{\min_xL(x,\lambda)}_{l(\lambda)} \qquad\text{(dual problem)}$$

The primal problem is, by construction, exactly the original constrained problem $\min_xf(x)$ s.t. $g(x)\le0$. The dual problem swaps the order of min and max, and (perhaps surprisingly) is *always* a **convex** problem, even when the primal isn't:

> **Theorem.** The dual problem is convex (objective concave, constraints convex), even if the primal is non-convex. ($L(x,\lambda)$ is linear in $\lambda$; $l(\lambda)=\min_xL(x,\lambda)$ is a pointwise minimization of linear functions, hence concave.)

**Weak duality** always holds: $l(\lambda)\le p^*$ for any $\lambda\ge0$ (the dual function is always a *lower bound* on the true optimum $p^*$, since swapping $\min$/$\max$ can only decrease the result in general, $\min_x\max_y \ge \max_y\min_x$). This means the dual gives you a certified lower bound even if you can't solve the (possibly non-convex) primal directly.

> **Theorem (Slater's condition $\Rightarrow$ strong duality).** If the primal is convex, and there exists an interior point $x$ with $g_i(x)<0$ for all $i$ (**Slater's condition**), then **strong duality** holds: $\max_{\lambda\ge0}\min_xL(x,\lambda) = \min_x\max_{\lambda\ge0}L(x,\lambda)$, i.e. the primal and dual optimal values coincide exactly.

## Summary

- The general NLP is $\min_xf(x)$ s.t. $g(x)\le0,h(x)=0$; at an optimum, the cost gradient must be balanced by the active constraint gradients.
- The full KKT theorem has four named conditions: stationarity (force balance), primal feasibility, dual feasibility ($\lambda\ge0$), and complementarity ($\lambda_ig_i(x)=0$, packaging active/inactive cases into one equation).
- The Lagrangian $L=f+\lambda^\top g+\kappa^\top h$ reproduces KKT via $\min_xL$ (stationarity) and $\max_{\lambda\ge0,\kappa}L$ (the rest); optima are saddle points of $L$.
- The dual problem $\max_{\lambda\ge0}\min_xL(x,\lambda)$ is always convex and always a lower bound on the primal (weak duality); under Slater's condition (primal convex + a strictly-interior point), strong duality holds and the bound is exact.
