## Why this matters

Least-squares objectives are everywhere in robotics: trajectory optimization costs, sensor calibration, system identification, and the convex-decomposition manipulation planner from your own research are all, underneath, repeatedly solving least-squares-flavored subproblems. Gauss-Newton is the standard workhorse for exactly this reason: it gets most of Newton's fast convergence without needing the true, expensive Hessian.

## Gauss-Newton: exploiting least-squares structure

When the objective has the specific form $f(x)=\tfrac12\|r(x)\|^2$ (a sum of squared residuals), there's a cheap, structured Hessian approximation available.

<ExerciseCard id="OPT-02-ex2-gauss-newton-derivation" />

The Gauss-Newton approximation $H(x)\approx J(x)^\top J(x)$ has a big practical advantage beyond just being cheaper to compute: $J^\top J$ is automatically positive semi-definite (for any real matrix $J$), so unlike the true Hessian, it can never be indefinite in a way that would send a naive Newton step in a wrong (ascending) direction. This is a large part of why Gauss-Newton is preferred over plain Newton for least-squares problems even when the true Hessian is affordable.

## Worked example

Let $r(x) = \begin{pmatrix}x_1-2\\x_2-3\\x_1+x_2-4\end{pmatrix}$ (linear, so $J$ is constant: $J=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$), starting at $x_0=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}$. Then $r(x_0)=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-3\\-4\end{pmatrix}$, $J^\top J = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $J^\top r(x_0) = \begin{pmatrix}-6\\-7\end{pmatrix}$, giving $\delta = -(J^\top J)^{-1}J^\top r(x_0) \approx \begin{pmatrix}1.667\\2.667\end{pmatrix}$.

*(Verified with NumPy against the true least-squares solution via `lstsq`: they match exactly, since Gauss-Newton is exact, not approximate, whenever the residual $r(x)$ is linear.)*

## Quasi-Newton (BFGS) and conjugate gradient, briefly

Two other strategies avoid the exact Hessian without relying on least-squares structure:

- **Quasi-Newton methods (BFGS)** build up an approximation of the Hessian (or its inverse) incrementally, using only the gradients observed at each step, no second derivatives computed at all. Each step's gradient difference gives one new piece of curvature information, refining the approximation as optimization proceeds.
- **Conjugate gradient (CG)** never forms an explicit Hessian approximation at all; it only ever needs the Hessian's *action on a vector* ($Hv$, cheap to compute directly for many problems without forming $H$), and constructs a sequence of search directions that are mutually "conjugate" (non-interfering) under $H$, converging in at most $n$ steps for an exact quadratic.

Both are covered at this conceptual level in this course; their full derivations are a natural next step if you need them for a specific research problem, but aren't required for the rest of this curriculum.

## Summary

- The exact Hessian costs $O(n^2)$ memory and $O(n^3)$ to solve with, impractical for large problems.
- For least-squares objectives, $H(x) = J^\top J + \sum_i r_i \nabla^2 r_i$; Gauss-Newton drops the second term, exact when residuals are linear, and a good approximation near a good fit generally.
- $J^\top J$ is always positive semi-definite, an important practical advantage over the true Hessian.
- BFGS and CG are alternative ways to get curvature information cheaply, without least-squares structure: BFGS builds an approximation from gradient history, CG avoids forming a Hessian approximation at all.
