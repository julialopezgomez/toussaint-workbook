## Why this matters

Legs make DYN-01's general dynamics $M(q)\ddot q+F(q,\dot q)=u$ genuinely harder in a specific way: contacts switch on and off (a foot lifts, another lands), and during the swing phase, the moving leg is entirely **unactuated** by ground contact. This module covers the two classical answers to "how do you even stand up, let alone walk," and where each one's real limitation is.

## Statically stable walking

**Walking** is defined as: at every instant, at least one foot is on the ground (contrast **running**, where there are instants with no ground contact at all). Walking has two phases: **double-support** (two feet down, typically *statically* stable) and **single-support** (one foot down, typically statically *unstable*).

A pose is **statically stable** iff the robot could simply hold that pose, indefinitely, without falling, which happens exactly when:

$$\text{statically stable} \iff \text{CoG (center of gravity) projected onto the ground lies within the support polygon}$$

where the **support polygon** is the convex hull of all current foot-ground contact points. (A useful intuition, straight from the source: try walking as slowly as possible while still taking normal-length steps, you'll find yourself needing to shift your weight noticeably over the stance foot before lifting the other, exactly maintaining this condition throughout.)

## Zero Moment Point (ZMP)

Static stability is a strong, conservative condition; **dynamic** stability (the robot can be walking, not just standing) needs a genuinely dynamic criterion. Vukobratović's **Zero Moment Point** is the classical answer, defined as the ground point $p_\text{ZMP}$ for which the net moment of inertial and gravitational forces has no horizontal component:

$$\sum_i r_i\times f_i + I_i\dot w_i+w_i\times I_iw_i \overset{!}{=}(0,0,*)^\top, \qquad r_i:=p_i-p_\text{ZMP}$$

(with $f_i$ the force, $I_i$ the inertia tensor, and $w_i$ the angular velocity of body $i$; $(0,0,*)$ meaning the first two, horizontal, components vanish, and the third, vertical, component is unconstrained.) **If the ZMP remains within the support polygon, the robot is dynamically stable**: intuitively, this is exactly the condition under which the ground can supply whatever reaction force/moment is needed without the foot needing to rotate about its edge (which would mean tipping over).

**Why ZMP matters practically**: ZMP-stability is a "rescue" that lets conventional control apply directly. If the ZMP stays inside the support polygon, the foot can be treated as *virtually glued* to the ground, meaning the whole robot behaves like a **conventional, fully-actuated arm** anchored at the stance foot: exactly DYN-03's operational-space control machinery applies unmodified, since every joint acceleration $\ddot q$ becomes freely commandable again.

<ExerciseCard id="MANIP-02-ex2-zmp-fully-actuated-conceptual" />

**Limitations, stated honestly**: humans don't actually walk with ZMP-stability throughout a stride (we deliberately roll onto the toe or heel, putting the ZMP right at the edge of the support polygon, not safely inside it); and the ZMP framework can't even describe robots with point feet (e.g. walking on stilts), since there's no foot area for a "support polygon" to be defined over in the first place.

## Passive dynamic walking

An entirely different philosophy: instead of fighting underactuation with ZMP-based control, **embrace it**. The **Compass Gait** is the simplest passive walker: a planar biped, configuration $q=(\theta_s,\theta_{ns})$ (stance and non-stance leg angles), that can walk down a gentle slope with **zero actuation at all**, purely from gravity and its own dynamics.

<ExerciseCard id="MANIP-02-ex3-passive-dynamics-connection" />

During the swing phase, the equations of motion are exactly DYN-01's general form, specialized to the unactuated case:

$$M(q)\ddot q+C(q,\dot q)\dot q+G(q)=0$$

(no analytic solution exists in general, but the numeric phase-space trajectory is well studied). Walking then requires switching between two consecutive swing phases at **impact** (when the swing leg touches down), modeled with a standard set of simplifying assumptions: no rebound or slipping at touchdown; the stance leg lifts off without any force interaction; the impact is instantaneous, representable by an impulsive force; velocities can jump discontinuously at impact, but the configuration itself cannot; and actuators (having finite force) cannot contribute anything during an instantaneous impact.

<ExerciseCard id="MANIP-02-ex4-hybrid-dynamics-conceptual" />

This switching between smooth continuous dynamics (the swing phase ODE above) and discrete, instantaneous events (impact) is what makes legged locomotion a genuinely **hybrid dynamical system**, distinct from the purely-continuous dynamics DYN-01 through DYN-06 assumed throughout. Passive dynamic walking is minimally actuated and remarkably efficient, but fragile to disturbances and terrain variation; more recent work (compliant/elastic legs, reinforcement learning directly on the walking policy, e.g. "Learning to Walk in 20 Minutes" using policy-gradient methods) tries to recover some of that efficiency while adding back enough robustness for real, imperfect terrain.

## Summary

- **Statically stable** walking requires the CoG's ground projection to stay within the support polygon (convex hull of foot contacts); walking has double-support (typically stable) and single-support (typically unstable) phases.
- The **Zero Moment Point** generalizes this to dynamic stability: if the ZMP stays within the support polygon, the stance foot can be treated as glued to the ground, letting a legged robot be controlled exactly like DYN-03's fully-actuated arm; humans don't actually maintain this throughout a stride, and it can't describe point-feet robots at all.
- **Passive dynamic walking** (the Compass Gait) embraces underactuation instead: the swing phase is exactly DYN-01's general dynamics form with $u=0$, switching between phases at instantaneous, impulsive impacts.
- This phase-switching makes legged locomotion a **hybrid dynamical system**, combining smooth continuous dynamics with discrete impact events, an important limitation of the purely-continuous DYN framework used so far.
