Seeing that unification explicitly, and a brief, honest look at where the "just define an objective and optimize" picture gets genuinely hard (value alignment), is worth more than another full derivation.

## The basic decision-theoretic formalism

Decision theory combines *probabilities* and *utilities*: a **decision variable** $a\in\mathcal{A}$, a (possibly stochastic) **outcome** $y\sim P(y\mid a)$, and a **utility function** $U:y\mapsto U(y)\in\mathbb{R}$. The **principle of maximal expected utility**: a rational agent chooses

$$a^* = \operatorname*{argmax}_a\ \mathbb{E}_{P(y\mid a)}\{U(y)\}$$

This is exactly RL-01's MDP objective, specialized to a single decision: $a\to$ policy, $y\to$ trajectory, $U\to$ discounted return. The **von Neumann-Morgenstern theorem** (1944) gives this principle real teeth: if an agent's preferences over outcomes ($y\preceq y'$) satisfy a short list of consistency axioms (orderable, transitive, continuous, and a few more technical conditions), then a utility function $U$ *provably exists* such that $y\preceq y'\iff U(y)\leq U(y')$, and the utility of an uncertain outcome is exactly its expected utility, $\mathbb{E}\{U(y)\}=\sum_yP(y)U(y)$. (Multiple different $U$ can represent the same preferences; the theorem guarantees *some* $U$ exists, not a unique one.)

**A common misconception worth naming directly**: "AI/economics assumes agents are rational, but humans aren't optimal, so the framework is limiting." This misreads what "optimal" means here: optimality principles aren't a claim about how any *particular* agent actually behaves, they're a **descriptive tool**, a formal way of characterizing a system's behavior (as physics describes motion via least-action principles, or economics describes markets via utility-maximizing agents), useful precisely because *any* consistent behavior can be cast as optimal under *some* utility function.

## Risk aversion and risk seeking

Utility need not be linear in outcome. For $y\in\mathbb{R}$ representing money, a **concave** $U(y)$ makes an agent **risk-averse** (prefers a certain amount over a gamble with the same expected value); a **convex** $U(y)$ makes it **risk-seeking**.

## Decision networks: MDPs and ML, unified

A **Decision Network** (influence diagram) is a DAG with three node types: **decision** variables $a_i$ (set by a policy $\pi_i$: parents $\mapsto a_i$), **random** variables $x_i$ (drawn $\sim P(x_i\mid\text{parents})$), and **utility** nodes $u_i$ (computed $=U_i(\text{parents})$); the objective is to choose every $\pi_i$ to maximize $\mathbb{E}\{\sum_iu_i\}$.

<ExerciseCard id="RL-07-ex2-mdp-as-decision-network-conceptual" />

**Supervised ML is a decision network too**, with a genuinely illuminating structure: given data $D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}$ and a new query $x'$, decide a prediction $\hat y'$ to minimize the expected **loss** $l(\hat y',y')$:

$$\hat y'^* = \operatorname*{argmin}_{\hat y'}\ \mathbb{E}_{p(y'\mid x')}\{l(\hat y',y')\}$$

called **Risk Minimization** in Statistical Learning Theory (a *minimization*, the mirror image of maximal-expected-utility, with $U=-l$). Since $p(y'\mid x')$ is unknown, it's approximated by a Monte Carlo average over the training data itself, $\mathbb{E}_{p(x',y')}\{l(f_\theta(x'),y')\}\approx\frac1n\sum_il(f_\theta(x_i),y_i)$, called **Empirical Risk Minimization**, exactly ML-02's loss-minimization framing (ridge regression, logistic regression), now seen as one specific instance of decision theory's general expected-utility maximization, applied to a *single-step*, non-sequential decision (choosing $\theta$ once from $D$, unlike RL-01's sequential MDP decisions).

## Beyond fixed tasks: information gain and intrinsic motivation

Not every objective is extrinsic reward. **Information gain**: choose the decision expected to most reduce uncertainty about something you don't yet know, $\operatorname*{argmax}_aH(p(\mu_a\mid D))-\mathbb{E}_{r\mid a}\{H(p(\mu_a\mid r,a,D))\}$ (entropy before minus expected entropy after, PROB-03's entropy), used in active learning (which point to label next) and next-best-view planning (which camera pose reveals the most about an unknown shape).

**Curiosity/intrinsic rewards** reward an agent for *learning*, not just task completion, but naively rewarding "high prediction error" fails badly: an agent rewarded for being surprised gravitates toward genuinely unpredictable (not learnable) stimuli, the "permanently watching white noise TV" failure mode, since white noise is maximally surprising *forever*, no matter how much you watch. The fix: reward **predictor improvement** (how much your predictions got *better* recently), not raw prediction error; white noise offers zero learning progress despite maximal surprise, correctly extinguishing the reward for watching it.

## Value alignment

If "optimality" is just a description of *some* objective, the hard question becomes: **which objective**? Stuart Russell's concern: as AI systems become more capable optimizers, badly-specified objectives become increasingly dangerous, not because optimization itself is wrong, but because **humans are bad at fully specifying what they actually want**, and a sufficiently powerful optimizer will find whatever technically satisfies a stated objective, including unintended, undesirable ways of doing so. Russell's proposed fix: rather than a system optimizing a *fixed*, human-specified objective, it should **infer** human preferences from behavior, maintaining substantial uncertainty about what those preferences really are (avoiding overconfident overfitting to limited, noisy behavioral evidence). This remains an open, actively-researched problem (game-theoretic formalizations like Cooperative Inverse RL are one of several attempts to make it rigorous), presented here at a conceptual level only, matching this module's own lighter scope.

## Summary

- Decision theory's core: a decision $a$, a stochastic outcome $y\sim P(y\mid a)$, a utility $U(y)$, and the principle of maximal expected utility; the von Neumann-Morgenstern theorem shows consistent preferences imply *some* utility function exists.
- **Risk aversion/seeking** correspond to concave/convex utility functions over outcomes.
- **Decision networks** generalize both MDPs (RL-01) and supervised ML (empirical risk minimization, ML-02) as instances of the identical formalism, differing in whether the decision process is sequential or single-step.
- **Information gain** and **curiosity/intrinsic motivation** are task-independent objectives; naive prediction-error curiosity fails (white-noise trap), fixed by rewarding *predictor improvement* instead.
- **Value alignment** is the open problem of specifying good objectives at all, given that humans are bad at fully specifying what they want; Russell's proposal is inferring preferences with appropriate uncertainty rather than fixing a hand-written objective.
