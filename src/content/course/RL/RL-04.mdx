## Why this matters

RL-03's Q-learning update is a single-sample nudge to a table, $Q(s,a)$. To handle continuous states/actions with ML-04's neural networks, both $Q(s,a)$ and $\pi(s)$ need to become genuine SGD-trainable functions with real loss functions and real gradients, not table updates. This module derives both losses: the **Bellman residual** (turns Q-learning into supervised regression) and the **policy gradient theorem** (the mathematical core of essentially every modern deep-RL algorithm, DDPG, PPO, SAC included).

## From table updates to loss functions: the Bellman residual

Storing a full replay buffer $\mathcal{D}=\{(s_i,a_i,r_i,s_{i+1})\}$ of all past experience (rather than discarding each transition after one TD update), Q-learning's per-sample TD error becomes recognizable as a gradient step on a genuine squared-error loss:

$$B_\mathcal{D}^\pi = \sum_{(s,a,r,s')\in\mathcal{D}}\big[r+\gamma\bar Q(s',\pi(s'))-Q(s,a)\big]^2$$

<ExerciseCard id="RL-04-ex1-bellman-residual-conceptual" />

This is now an ordinary regression problem (ML-02/ML-04's territory): train a neural network $Q_\theta(s,a)$ to minimize $B_\mathcal{D}^\pi$ via SGD (OPT-05), typically on a random mini-batch $\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{D}$ each step rather than the whole buffer. $\bar Q$ is a **delayed** (slowly-updated, low-pass-filtered) copy of $Q_\theta$'s own parameters, used only in the target, not the trainable network itself, a stabilization trick: training $Q_\theta$ against a target that also depends on the *current* $Q_\theta$ (a "moving target") is notoriously unstable, so freezing the target for a while breaks that feedback loop.

## The policy gradient theorem

Training $\pi_\theta$ directly maximizes expected return, $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim P_\theta}\{R(\xi)\}=\int P_\theta(\xi)R(\xi)\,d\xi$ (a trajectory-space integral: $\xi$ is a full trajectory, $R(\xi)=\sum_t\gamma^tr_t$ its total discounted return). Differentiating a probability-weighted integral directly is awkward ($P_\theta$ sits *inside* the integral, weighting $\xi$'s likelihood); the **log-derivative trick** fixes this:

<ExerciseCard id="RL-04-ex2-log-derivative-trick" />

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}J(\theta) = \int\frac{\partial P_\theta(\xi)}{\partial\theta}R(\xi)\,d\xi = \int P_\theta(\xi)\frac{\partial\log P_\theta(\xi)}{\partial\theta}R(\xi)\,d\xi = \mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim P_\theta}\Big\{\frac{\partial\log P_\theta(\xi)}{\partial\theta}R(\xi)\Big\}$$

**turning an unwieldy integral into an expectation you can Monte-Carlo estimate directly from sampled trajectories**, no need to differentiate through the environment's own (possibly unknown) dynamics at all. The trajectory probability factorizes as $P_\theta(\xi)=P(s_0)\prod_t\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$; only the $\pi_\theta$ factors depend on $\theta$, so:

<ExerciseCard id="RL-04-ex3-trajectory-factorization-derivation" />

$$\frac{\partial\log P_\theta(\xi)}{\partial\theta} = \sum_t\frac{\partial\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\partial\theta}$$

**the environment's own dynamics $P(s'\mid s,a)$ drop out of the gradient entirely**, exactly why policy gradient works even with a completely unknown/non-differentiable environment. Combining, and using a standard causality argument (a reward $r_{t'}$ occurring *before* time $t$ can't statistically depend on the action taken at $t$, so those cross terms vanish in expectation, leaving only $t'\geq t$ terms), gives the **Policy Gradient Theorem**:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim P_\theta}\Big\{\sum_t\gamma^t\Big[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big]\underbrace{\sum_{t'\geq t}\gamma^{t'-t}r_{t'}}_{Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t)}\Big\}}$$

Each term is "push up the log-probability of the action taken, weighted by how good it turned out to be" (its Q-value), exactly matching intuition: actions followed by high return get reinforced, actions followed by low return get suppressed.

For a **deterministic** policy $a=\pi_\theta(s)$, an analogous (simpler, since there's no expectation over stochastic actions to handle) derivation via the ordinary chain rule gives the **deterministic policy gradient**, $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_s\big\{\frac{\partial Q(s,a)}{\partial a}\big|_{a=\pi_\theta(s)}\frac{\partial\pi_\theta(s)}{\partial\theta}\big\}$ (the foundation of DDPG), needing a differentiable $Q(s,a)$ (exactly what the Bellman residual above trains) to supply $\partial Q/\partial a$.

## Deep RL in practice: interleaved training and stabilization tricks

Modern deep RL (**actor-critic** methods: DDPG, TD3, SAC) interleaves gradient steps on the Bellman residual (training the "critic" $Q_\theta$) with gradient steps on the policy gradient (training the "actor" $\pi_\theta$), while continuously collecting more data. A grab-bag of tricks stabilizes this in practice, each addressing a specific failure mode:

| Trick | Addresses |
|---|---|
| Replay buffer | Correlated, non-i.i.d. data from a single evolving trajectory |
| Delayed/low-pass-filtered target $\bar Q$ | The "moving target" instability of training against your own current estimate |
| Double Q-learning (two independent $Q_1,Q_2$, use $\min$) | Systematic overestimation bias in $\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$-style targets |
| Smoothed policy samples (clipped noise) | Exploiting sharp, unrealistic peaks in an imperfectly-learned $Q$ |
| Prioritized replay | Wasted gradient steps on already-well-fit transitions |

## A robotics-flavored note: why $Q$, not $V$?

For continuous action spaces, $Q(s,a)$ directly hands you a gradient w.r.t. $a$ (needed for the deterministic policy gradient above); working with $V(s)$ alone would additionally require learning an *inverse dynamics model* to figure out which action increases $V$, an extra, error-compounding step. This is precisely the flip side of DYN-03/DYN-04's control-theory convention, where the **Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation** is stated purely in terms of $V(x,t)$ (control theory typically *does* assume a known, differentiable dynamics model $f(x,u)$, so the extra step Q avoids in RL is trivial there): the choice of $V$ vs. $Q$ tracks whether the dynamics model is known and differentiable, exactly the model-based/model-free distinction RL-05 formalizes next.

## Summary

- The **Bellman residual** $B_\mathcal{D}^\pi$ generalizes Q-learning's single-sample TD update into a genuine squared-error loss, trainable by ordinary SGD on a replay buffer; a delayed target $\bar Q$ stabilizes the resulting "moving target" problem.
- The **policy gradient theorem**, derived via the log-derivative trick, turns $\partial J/\partial\theta$ into a sampling-friendly expectation, $\mathbb{E}\{\sum_t\gamma^t[\partial\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\partial\theta]\,Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t)\}$, with the environment's own dynamics dropping out entirely.
- The **deterministic policy gradient** needs a differentiable $Q(s,a)$ to supply $\partial Q/\partial a$, exactly what the Bellman residual trains.
- Modern actor-critic deep RL interleaves both losses' gradient steps with data collection, stabilized by a standard set of tricks (replay buffer, delayed targets, double Q-learning, and more).
