## Why this matters

RL-03's Q-learning used $\epsilon$-greedy exploration without ever justifying it: act randomly some fraction of the time, hope it's enough. This module makes exploration itself the object of study, exactly the exploration-exploitation problem OPT-06 formalized for bandits, now in the harder sequential-decision setting where visiting a promising-looking state might itself require a whole plan to get there.

## What can be learned from RL data?

Given data $\mathcal{D}_t=\{(s_i,a_i,r_i,s_{i+1})\}_{i=0}^t$, three distinct strategies:

- **Model-based RL**: learn $\hat P(s'\mid s,a)$ and $\hat R(s,a)$ from data, then apply RL-02's value/Q-iteration (or planning) to the *learned* model.
- **Model-free RL**: learn $V^*(s)$ or $Q^*(s,a)$ directly from data (RL-03's TD-learning/Q-learning), never explicitly representing $P$ or $R$.
- **Policy search**: estimate the policy gradient directly (RL-04), or use black-box/evolutionary search, skipping value functions entirely.

<ExerciseCard id="RL-05-ex1-taxonomy-conceptual" />

This taxonomy matters directly for exploration: **model-based RL makes "planned exploration" far easier**, since you have an explicit model to plan a path toward an uncertain state with; model-free methods, knowing only $Q(s,a)$, have no such map, "visiting a state with high uncertainty is itself a planning problem" that model-free methods have no natural way to solve.

## Beyond $\epsilon$-greedy: optimistic initialization and UCB

$\epsilon$-greedy explores *undirected*: it doesn't distinguish "an action I've barely tried" from "an action I've tried a thousand times and it's still mediocre," both get the same random-exploration probability $\epsilon$. Two fixes, both already familiar from OPT-06:

- **Optimistic initialization**: initialize $Q(s,a)$ to a deliberately high value (e.g. the theoretical maximum $Q(s,a)=R_\text{max}/(1-\gamma)$, the most reward achievable forever, discounted), so every action *looks* attractive until experience proves otherwise, naturally driving the agent to try everything at least once.

<ExerciseCard id="RL-05-ex2-optimistic-init-numeric" />

- **UCB-style exploration**: if you can estimate a confidence bound $\sigma(s,a)$ for $Q(s,a)$ (e.g. via bootstrap estimates), choose actions using $Q(s,a)+\beta\sigma(s,a)$, **exactly OPT-06's UCB1 formula**, $\mu_a+\beta\sqrt{2\ln n/n_a}$, applied to $Q$-values instead of bandit-arm means: the exploration bonus shrinks as $(s,a)$ gets visited more, automatically transitioning from exploration to exploitation.

<ExerciseCard id="RL-05-ex3-ucb-connection-conceptual" />

## R-max: provably efficient model-based exploration

**R-max** (Brafman & Tennenholtz, 2002) makes optimism concrete and provably efficient in the model-based setting. Track a visit count $c(s,a)$; estimate $\hat R,\hat P$ from data only once $(s,a)$ is "known" (visited at least $m$ times); until then, use a strictly optimistic reward:

$$R_\text{R-max}(s,a) = \begin{cases}R(s,a) & c(s,a)\geq m \quad\text{($s,a$ known)}\\ R_\text{max} & c(s,a)<m \quad\text{($s,a$ unknown)}\end{cases}$$

<ExerciseCard id="RL-05-ex4-rmax-mechanism-derivation" />

Planning (RL-02's value/Q-iteration) against $R_\text{R-max}$ means every insufficiently-visited state-action pair looks maximally attractive, so the resulting optimal policy *automatically* seeks out unvisited/under-visited $(s,a)$ pairs until they become "known," at which point planning naturally shifts toward genuine exploitation, all without any separate exploration bonus or hand-tuned $\epsilon$. R-max is **PAC-MDP** (probably-approximately-correct): it provably reaches a near-optimal policy within a number of exploratory steps polynomial in the size of the MDP.

**KWIK-R-max** (Li, Littman, Walsh & Strehl, 2011) extends this to structured (parametric) transition models with $n\ll|\mathcal{S}|$ parameters, needing only polynomially-many-in-$n$ samples rather than polynomially-many-in-$|\mathcal{S}|$, a substantial efficiency gain whenever the model has real structure to exploit.

## Bayesian RL: the theoretically optimal (but intractable) answer

There *is* a formally optimal solution to exploration-exploitation: **belief planning**. Maintain a belief $b(m)$, a probability distribution over which MDP $m$ you're actually in (updated via Bayes' rule after every observation, ML-05's Bayesian machinery generalized from parameters to whole models), and solve

$$V^\pi(b,s) = R(s,\pi(b,s))+\int_{b',s'}P(b',s'\mid b,s,\pi(b,s))\,V^\pi(b',s')$$

The **Bayes-optimal policy** $\pi^*=\operatorname*{argmax}_\pi V^\pi(b,s)$ provably achieves the best possible expected reward under the prior over MDPs, genuinely solving exploration-exploitation optimally, at the cost of being tractable only for very simple problems (the belief space $b$ is itself a probability distribution over models, generally infinite-dimensional). R-max and KWIK-R-max are, in effect, practical approximations that capture most of belief planning's "seek out what you don't know" spirit without its intractability.

## Summary

- RL data supports three strategies: **model-based** (learn $P,R$, then plan), **model-free** (learn $V^*/Q^*$ directly), and **policy-search** (estimate the policy gradient or use black-box search); model-based RL makes directed exploration far easier.
- **Optimistic initialization** and **UCB-style exploration** ($Q(s,a)+\beta\sigma(s,a)$, exactly OPT-06's UCB1 formula) both make under-explored actions look attractive until proven otherwise.
- **R-max** treats every insufficiently-visited $(s,a)$ as maximally rewarding until visited $m$ times, giving provably-efficient (PAC-MDP) exploration with no separate exploration bonus needed; **KWIK-R-max** extends this to structured models.
- **Bayesian RL** (belief planning) is the theoretically optimal answer to exploration-exploitation, generally intractable but the conceptual benchmark R-max-style methods approximate.
