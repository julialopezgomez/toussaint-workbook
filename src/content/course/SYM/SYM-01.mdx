## Syntax and semantics

A **logic** is a formal language (its **syntax** defines valid sentences) together with a definition of truth (its **semantics** defines when a sentence is true in a **model**, a world/state description). Propositional logic's syntax: atomic sentences are `true`, `false`, or a proposition symbol ($P,Q,R,\ldots$); complex sentences combine them via $\neg,\wedge,\vee,\Rightarrow,\Leftrightarrow$. Semantics: each model assigns true/false to every proposition symbol, and truth of a complex sentence follows the standard recursive rules ($\neg S$ true iff $S$ false; $S_1\wedge S_2$ true iff both true; $S_1\Rightarrow S_2$ true iff $S_1$ false or $S_2$ true; etc.).

## Entailment, equivalence, validity, satisfiability

Writing $M(\alpha):=\{m:\alpha(m)=\text{true}\}$ (the set of models where $\alpha$ holds):

- **Entailment** $\alpha\models\beta$: $M(\alpha)\subseteq M(\beta)$ (every model of $\alpha$ is also a model of $\beta$).
- **Equivalence** $\alpha\equiv\beta$: $\alpha\models\beta$ and $\beta\models\alpha$.
- **Valid**: true in every model (e.g. $A\vee\neg A$). **Unsatisfiable**: true in no model (e.g. $A\wedge\neg A$).
- A **knowledge base (KB)** is a conjunction of sentences; an **inference procedure** $i$ derives $KB\vdash_i\alpha$. $i$ is **sound** iff $KB\vdash_i\alpha\Rightarrow KB\models\alpha$ (it never derives anything false); **complete** iff $KB\models\alpha\Rightarrow KB\vdash_i\alpha$ (it can derive everything true). Useful reformulation: $KB\models\alpha$ iff $(KB\wedge\neg\alpha)$ is unsatisfiable, the basis of proof-by-contradiction inference methods.

## Horn clauses and Modus Ponens

A **literal** is $A$ or $\neg A$; a **clause** is a disjunction of literals; a **Horn clause** is either a single symbol or $(\text{conjunction of symbols})\Rightarrow\text{symbol}$ (at most one positive literal). A KB in **Horn form** (a conjunction of Horn clauses) admits a simple, complete inference rule, **Modus Ponens**:

$$\frac{\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n,\quad\alpha_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_n\Rightarrow\beta}{\beta}$$

("given $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$ all true, and the rule that their conjunction implies $\beta$, conclude $\beta$".) Applying Modus Ponens repeatedly, always deriving new facts from rules whose premises are already known, is **forward chaining**; running it backward from a query, checking whether its premises can themselves be established, is **backward chaining** (developed fully for the richer first-order case in SYM-02). Both run in time *linear* in KB size for Horn-form KBs, a major reason Horn clauses are so widely used in practice (e.g. Prolog).

<ExerciseCard id="SYM-01-ex3-modus-ponens-derivation" />

For KBs not restricted to Horn form, **resolution** (converting sentences to Conjunctive Normal Form, a conjunction of disjunctive clauses, then repeatedly combining clauses containing complementary literals) is sound and complete for all of propositional logic, a strictly more general (but more expensive) tool than Modus Ponens; SYM-02 develops resolution's first-order generalization in more depth, so it isn't developed further here.

## Summary

- A logic's **syntax** defines sentences; its **semantics** defines truth under a **model**; **entailment** ($\alpha\models\beta$) means every model of $\alpha$ is a model of $\beta$.
- **Soundness** (never derives anything false) and **completeness** (can derive everything true) are the two properties any inference procedure is judged by.
- **Horn clauses** (at most one positive literal) admit **Modus Ponens**-based forward/backward chaining, sound, complete for Horn KBs, and linear-time.
- **Resolution** is the more general (but more expensive) tool for arbitrary propositional sentences, developed further in SYM-02's first-order setting.
