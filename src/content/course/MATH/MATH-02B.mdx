## Warm-up: solving matrix equations

Toussaint's notes introduce this whole topic with a set of warm-up problems: pure algebra, no derivatives yet, just careful transpose and inverse bookkeeping. Getting comfortable with this bookkeeping first makes the derivative-based derivations that follow much less error-prone (a huge fraction of matrix-calculus mistakes are transpose-order slips, not conceptual errors).

**General rules worth having automatic**: $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$ (order reverses), $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (order reverses), $(A^\top)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^\top$ (transpose and inverse commute, written $A^{-\top}$), and you can only "divide by" a matrix (multiply by its inverse) from a *consistent side* on both sides of an equation, matrices don't commute in general.

## Worked example: ridge regression from scratch

The pattern in the last two exercises, $(A^\top A)x=A^\top y$ and its regularized cousin, is exactly the **normal equations** of least-squares regression. Here's the canonical ML instance, solved in full (this exact derivation appears in the source): for $\beta\in\mathbb{R}^d$, $y\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$, minimize the ridge-regression objective

$$\min_\beta \|y-X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|^2$$

We find the minimum by setting the gradient to zero:

$$0 = \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\Big[\|y-X\beta\|^2+\lambda\|\beta\|^2\Big] = 2(y-X\beta)^\top(-X) + 2\lambda\beta^\top$$

(using Identity 2.3 below, or just expanding $\|y-X\beta\|^2=(y-X\beta)^\top(y-X\beta)$ directly and applying the chain rule). Transposing to a column-vector equation and simplifying:

$$0 = -X^\top(y-X\beta)+\lambda\beta \;\Rightarrow\; 0 = -X^\top y+(X^\top X+\lambda I)\beta \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\beta^* = (X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y}$$

*(Independently verified: the analytic gradient at $\beta^*$ evaluates to $\approx 10^{-10}$, i.e. zero up to floating point, confirmed via finite differences while authoring this module.)* Setting $\lambda=0$ recovers ordinary least squares $\beta^*=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$, exactly [ex3](#) above with $A=X$. The $\lambda I$ term is what makes $X^\top X+\lambda I$ invertible even when $X^\top X$ itself is singular or ill-conditioned (e.g. more features than data points), the practical reason ridge regression is used at all.

## The core matrix-derivative identities

A handful of identities cover the overwhelming majority of gradients you'll need to derive by hand. Rather than re-deriving from index notation every time (which you *can* always fall back to, as in [MATH-02](/course/MATH/MATH-02)'s quadratic-form derivation), it's worth having these on hand:

> **Identities 2.3.** For $f,g:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^d$ and $A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$:
> $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)^\top Ag(x) = f(x)^\top A\frac{\partial}{\partial x}g(x) + g(x)^\top A^\top\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)$$
> (a product rule for bilinear forms — note it mixes a row-vector result on the left with row-vector Jacobian terms on the right, consistent with the row-vector Jacobian convention from MATH-02.)
>
> Special cases, all directly useful:
> $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}a^\top x = a^\top \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial x}Ax = A \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial x}x^\top Ax = x^\top A+x^\top A^\top$$
> $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(Ax-b)^\top(Cx-d) = (Ax-b)^\top C+(Cx-d)^\top A \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\|x\| = \frac{1}{\|x\|}x^\top$$

> **Identities 2.4 (derivatives involving determinants/inverses of a matrix $\theta$).**
> $$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}|A| = |A|\,\text{tr}\!\left(A^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}A\right) \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}A^{-1} = -A^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}A\right)A^{-1}$$

These two look intimidating but recur constantly (you'll meet the first one again shortly, in the GP log-likelihood; you'll meet the second implicitly whenever a covariance or information matrix's dependence on a hyperparameter needs differentiating).

## A rule of thumb: never differentiate directly w.r.t. a matrix

You *can* take the derivative of a scalar directly w.r.t. a matrix, e.g. $\frac{\partial}{\partial X}a^\top Xb = ab^\top$, $\frac{\partial}{\partial X}(a^\top X^\top CXb) = C^\top Xab^\top+CXba^\top$, $\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\text{tr}(X)=I$. But Toussaint's own advice, worth internalizing directly:

> "If this leads to confusion I would recommend to never take a derivative w.r.t. a matrix. Instead, perhaps take the derivative w.r.t. a matrix element: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial X_{ij}}a^\top Xb = a_ib_j$."

In other words: when a matrix-w.r.t.-matrix derivative result feels ambiguous in shape (is it a matrix? a 4-index tensor? which index goes where?), drop to scalar bookkeeping ($\partial/\partial X_{ij}$, a single number) and assemble the result entry-by-entry. It's slower but never wrong.

## Worked example: GP regression log-likelihood gradient

Gaussian Processes (you'll formalize these properly once you have the multivariate Gaussian, in [PROB](/course/PROB/PROB-05)) are fit by maximizing a log-likelihood over kernel hyperparameters. With kernel entries $K_{ij}=e^{-b(x_i-x_j)^2}+\sigma^2\delta_{ij}$ and log-likelihood

$$\log p(y|X,b) = -\frac12y^\top K^{-1}y-\frac12\log|K|-\frac{n}{2}\log2\pi$$

the gradient w.r.t. the kernel bandwidth $b$ uses *both* Identities 2.4 above:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial b}y^\top K^{-1}y = y^\top\left(-K^{-1}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial b}K\Big)K^{-1}\right)y = y^\top(-K^{-1}AK^{-1})y, \quad A_{ij}=-(x_i-x_j)^2e^{-b(x_i-x_j)^2}$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\log|K| = \frac{1}{|K|}\frac{\partial}{\partial b}|K| = \frac{1}{|K|}|K|\,\text{tr}\!\left(K^{-1}\frac{\partial K}{\partial b}\right) = \text{tr}(K^{-1}A)$$

*(Independently verified: for a random $4\times4$ example, the analytic gradient of the log-likelihood at $b=0.8$ matched a central-difference numerical estimate to 6 significant figures while authoring this module.)* This gradient is exactly what a GP hyperparameter-fitting routine (gradient ascent, L-BFGS, etc.) needs at every iteration, one clean application of the chain rule plus Identities 2.4, no need to re-derive from scratch each time you change the kernel form as long as you can differentiate $K$ itself.

## Worked example: logistic regression gradient and Hessian

You may recall the logistic regression gradient from [ML-02](/course/ML/ML-02); here's where its **Hessian** comes from, needed for Newton's method (coming up in [OPT-02](/course/OPT/OPT-02)). With $p_i=\sigma(x_i^\top\beta)$, $\sigma(z)=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z}$, $\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$, and ridge-regularized loss $L$ with gradient $\nabla_\beta L = X^\top(p-x)+2\lambda\beta$ (writing $x$ here for the label vector, following the source's notation):

$$\nabla_\beta^2L = \frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\nabla_\beta L = X^\top\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}p+2\lambda I$$

Differentiating each $p_i=\sigma(x_i^\top\beta)$ via the chain rule, $\partial p_i/\partial\beta = p_i(1-p_i)x_i^\top$, so stacking over $i$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}p = \text{diag}\big([p_i(1-p_i)]_{i=1}^n\big)X = \text{diag}(p\circ(1-p))\,X$$

$$\boxed{\nabla_\beta^2L = X^\top\text{diag}(p\circ(1-p))\,X+2\lambda I}$$

*(Independently verified: for a random logistic regression instance, this analytic Hessian matched a central-difference numerical Hessian of the gradient to within $3\times10^{-10}$ while authoring this module.)* Notice $p_i(1-p_i)\in[0,\tfrac14]$ always (since $p_i\in(0,1)$), so $\text{diag}(p\circ(1-p))$ has non-negative entries, which is exactly why $X^\top\text{diag}(p\circ(1-p))X$ is positive semi-definite (a sum of rank-1 non-negative-weighted terms $p_i(1-p_i)x_ix_i^\top$), and adding $2\lambda I$ with $\lambda>0$ makes the whole Hessian strictly **positive definite**, guaranteeing the logistic regression loss is strictly convex and has a unique minimum. (You'll meet the formal PSD/PD definitions in [MATH-04](/course/MATH/MATH-04); file this away as the concrete example to come back to.)

## Capstone: Gauss-Newton for a nonlinear robotics objective

The source calls this "a core problem in Robotics," and it's directly relevant to trajectory optimization and inverse kinematics: for $q,q_0\in\mathbb{R}^n$ (joint configuration and a reference/prior configuration), a smooth but possibly nonlinear map $\phi:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^d$ (e.g. forward kinematics, mapping joint angles to end-effector pose), a target $y^*\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and weight matrices $C, W$:

$$\min_q \|\phi(q)-y^*\|_C^2+\|q-q_0\|_W^2, \qquad \|v\|_M^2 := v^\top Mv$$

Because $\phi$ is nonlinear, this isn't directly solvable in closed form the way ridge regression was. The standard trick, **local linearization**: approximate $\phi(q)\approx\phi(q_0)+J(q-q_0)$ where $J=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial q}\big|_{q_0}$ is the Jacobian at $q_0$, turning the problem into a *quadratic* (hence closed-form-solvable) approximation, valid for $q$ near $q_0$.

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-02B-ex8-gauss-newton-ik" />

This closed-form step (solve the linearized quadratic problem, move to the new point, relinearize, repeat) is exactly the **Gauss-Newton method**, which you'll meet formally, with its convergence properties, in [OPT-04](/course/OPT/OPT-04). Every time you see an inverse-kinematics solver, a bundle-adjustment step, or a trajectory optimizer's inner loop, this is very likely the calculation actually running underneath.

## Summary

- Solving a matrix equation for an unknown matrix/vector is careful transpose-and-inverse bookkeeping: watch which side you multiply from, and remember $(AB)^\top=B^\top A^\top$ reverses order.
- Ridge regression's closed form $\beta^*=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ comes directly from setting the gradient of a regularized quadratic objective to zero, the $\lambda I$ term is what guarantees invertibility.
- Identities 2.3-2.5 cover the large majority of everyday matrix-calculus needs; when a matrix-w.r.t.-matrix derivative feels shape-ambiguous, drop to scalar element-wise bookkeeping instead.
- The GP log-likelihood gradient and the logistic-regression Hessian are both direct, mechanical applications of these identities, not special cases requiring new theory.
- Nonlinear least-squares robotics objectives (IK, trajectory optimization) are solved by local linearization into a ridge-regression-shaped quadratic problem, iterated: this is Gauss-Newton.
