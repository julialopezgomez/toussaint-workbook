## Why this matters

The SVD and eigendecomposition underlie PCA (dimensionality reduction), least-squares/pseudo-inverse solutions, matrix conditioning, low-rank approximation, and (via the power method) practical large-scale eigenvalue computation, all of which reappear throughout this course and your thesis work (covariance estimation, reachability analysis, spectral properties of dynamics linearizations). It's also one of the cleanest illustrations of MATH-03's theme: a matrix looks arbitrary in the standard basis, but in the *right* basis (the singular/eigen- directions), its action becomes as simple as possible: independent scalings along orthogonal axes.

## Matrices as row space / column space

A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ can be viewed as a collection of rows (each row dotted with $x$ picks up a component of $x$ within the row-spanned input space), or as a collection of columns (applying $A$ to $x$ linearly combines the columns, weighted by $x$'s entries, landing in the column-spanned output space). The problem: raw rows/columns are generally neither orthonormal nor guaranteed independent, so there's no clean, explicit description of exactly how $A$ transports the input space to the output space.

> **Definition 3.14 (rank).** $\text{rank}(f)=\text{rank}(A)$ of a transform $f$ or its matrix $A$ is the unique minimal $k$ such that $A$ can be written as a sum of $k$ rank-1 terms.
>
> **Definition 3.16 (row/column space, null space).** The **row space** of $A$ is $\text{span}\{v_i\}_{i=1}^{\text{rank}(A)}$ (the input fundamental space); the **input null space** $\mathcal{V}_\perp$ is its orthogonal complement, $v\in\mathcal{V}_\perp\Rightarrow f(v)=0$. Symmetrically, the **column space** is $\text{span}\{u_i\}_{i=1}^{\text{rank}(A)}$ (output fundamental space), with **output null space** $\mathcal{U}_\perp$ orthogonal to it.

## The SVD theorem

For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, there exists a rank $k \le \min(m,n)$, orthonormal vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$, orthonormal vectors $u_1,\dots,u_k\in\mathbb{R}^m$, and scalars $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_k > 0$, such that

$$A = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top = USV^\top, \qquad S=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1:k}),\ U=u_{1:k},\ V=v_{1:k}$$

Each term $\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$ projects $x$ onto input direction $v_i$, scales by $\sigma_i$, and places the result along output direction $u_i$, entirely independently of the other terms (since the $v_i$ are mutually orthogonal and the $u_i$ are mutually orthogonal). Put in one sentence: **every matrix $A$ can be expressed as a linear combination of only $k$ rank-1 "project-scale-unproject" matrices**, and rank-1 matrices are the most minimalistic kind of matrix there is, always of the form $uv^\top$ for some $u,v$.

> **Definition 3.15 (determinant).** $\det(A) = \pm\prod_{i=1}^n\sigma_i$ if $\text{rank}(A)=n=m$, else $0$ (the sign depends on whether the transform includes a reflection).

## Understanding $vv^\top$ as a projection

You already met this in [MATH-03B](/course/MATH/MATH-03B): the projection of $x$ onto a unit vector $v$ is $x_\| = vv^\top x$ (or $\frac{1}{\|v\|^2}v v^\top x$ if $v$ isn't normalized), and the projection matrix $vv^\top$ is symmetric, PSD, and rank 1. The SVD is directly built from this idea: it represents *any* matrix as a sum of "project [onto $v_i$] $\to$ scale [by $\sigma_i$] $\to$ unproject [into $u_i$]" pieces, each one a stretched version of exactly this rank-1 projection structure.

## SVD for symmetric matrices, and the eigendecomposition connection

You rarely need to invoke specialized SVD algorithms for small matrices, and the SVD is directly related to a decomposition you may already know: for a **symmetric** matrix $A$,

$$A = \sum_i\lambda_iv_iv_i^\top = V\Lambda V^\top, \qquad Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$$

i.e. the same $v_i$ are simultaneously the SVD's orthonormal directions *and* $A$'s eigenvectors, with $\lambda_i=\pm\sigma_i$ (positive if the corresponding eigenvalue is positive, since singular values are always non-negative by definition but eigenvalues can be negative). If $A$ is additionally positive semi-definite, every $\lambda_i\ge0$, so $\lambda_i=\sigma_i$ exactly and $U=V$.

## Worked example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$ (symmetric, chosen so the eigendecomposition is clean). Since $A$ is symmetric, $A^\top A = A^2$, and $A$'s own eigendecomposition gives the SVD directly (up to sign): eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda=4$ (eigenvector $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$) and $\lambda=2$ (eigenvector $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$). Both eigenvalues are positive, so the singular values are just $\sigma_1=4$, $\sigma_2=2$ (for a symmetric positive-definite matrix, singular values equal eigenvalues exactly, and $U=V$).

*(Verified with NumPy's `svd` while authoring this module: $S=[4.0, 2.0]$, matching exactly.)*

## The pseudo-inverse

For a symmetric matrix, the **pseudo-inverse** simply reverses the stretching along the same orthogonal projections:

$$A^\dagger = \sum_i\lambda_i^{-1}v_iv_i^\top = V\Lambda^{-1}V^\top, \qquad AA^\dagger=A^\dagger A=VV^\top$$

For a general (non-symmetric, possibly non-square) matrix, using the SVD $A=USV^\top$:

$$A^\dagger = \sum_i\sigma_i^{-1}v_iu_i^\top = VS^{-1}U^\top$$

If $A$ has full column rank ($k=n$), $A^\dagger=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ is the **left** pseudo-inverse ($A^\dagger A=I_n$), exactly the normal-equations solution from [MATH-02B](/course/MATH/MATH-02B)'s least-squares derivation. If $A$ has full row rank ($k=m$), $A^\dagger=A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}$ is the **right** pseudo-inverse ($AA^\dagger=I_m$). When $A$ isn't full rank, $A^\dagger y$ is still well-defined and gives the minimizer of $\|Ax-y\|^2$ closest to zero among the (infinitely many) minimizers.

## Covariance, and the SVD/PCA connection

For a zero-mean dataset $D=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^m$, $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the **covariance matrix** is $C=\frac1n\sum_ix_ix_i^\top=\frac1nX^\top X$ (with data matrix $X$ containing $x_i^\top$ as its $i$-th row). $C$ is symmetric PSD (a sum of PSD rank-1 terms $x_ix_i^\top$, each PSD since $v^\top x_ix_i^\top v=(x_i^\top v)^2\ge0$), so its eigendecomposition $C=\sum_i\lambda_iv_iv_i^\top$ always exists with all $\lambda_i\ge0$. Here $\lambda_i$ is exactly the **data variance along eigenvector $v_i$**, $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ the standard deviation along $v_i$, and $\sqrt{\lambda_i}v_i$ the principal-axis vectors that make up the ellipsoid typically used to illustrate a covariance. This is exactly **Principal Component Analysis**: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, sorted by eigenvalue, are the directions of maximal variance in the data, in order.

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-04-ex6-pca-variance" />

## Eigendecomposition, formally

> **Definition 3.18 (eigendecomposition).** The eigendecomposition (diagonalization) of $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, if it exists, is $A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}$, where $\Lambda=\text{diag}(\lambda_1,..,\lambda_n)$ and each column $q_i$ of $Q$ is an **eigenvector**.

Unlike the SVD, this is *not* a theorem, it's a definition, and it doesn't always exist. The eigenvalues are exactly the roots of the **characteristic polynomial** $p_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)$ (this has a root at $\lambda$ precisely because $A-\lambda I$ then has zero determinant, i.e. rank $<n$, i.e. there's a nonzero $x$ with $(A-\lambda I)x=0$, i.e. $Ax=\lambda x$). A matrix fails to be diagonalizable exactly when some eigenvalue's *algebraic* multiplicity (as a root of $p_A$) exceeds the dimension of its eigenvector span, geometrically, the eigenvectors for that eigenvalue don't span enough dimensions. Only a measure-zero subset of matrices are like this (random matrices are diagonalizable with probability 1); every symmetric matrix is always diagonalizable, with real eigenvalues, which is exactly the SVD-for-symmetric-matrices connection above.

## The power method: finding the largest eigenvalue

For large matrices, you don't want to compute the full characteristic polynomial. The **power method** finds the largest-magnitude eigenvalue/eigenvector iteratively: initialize $x$ randomly, then iterate

$$x \leftarrow Ax, \qquad x \leftarrow \frac{1}{\|x\|}x$$

If this converges, $x$ must be an eigenvector, with eigenvalue $\lambda=x^\top Ax$. If $A$ is diagonalizable and $x$ is initially a (generic) combination of all eigenvectors, $x$ converges to the eigenvector with the largest $|\lambda_i|$ (if the largest-magnitude eigenvalue is negative, $x$ flips sign every iteration rather than truly converging, but still identifies the right eigenvector/eigenvalue up to that alternation).

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-04-ex9-power-method-convergence" />

**A trick for the smallest eigenvalue** (assuming all eigenvalues are positive, e.g. a PSD matrix, a common setting in model fitting): initialize $x,y$ randomly and iterate

$$x\leftarrow Ax,\quad \lambda\leftarrow\|x\|,\quad x\leftarrow x/\lambda,\quad y\leftarrow(\lambda I-A)y,\quad y\leftarrow y/\|y\|$$

Then $y$ converges to the *smallest* eigenvector, and $\lambda-\|y\|$'s corresponding value converges to its eigenvalue. This works because $\lambda I - A$ (with $\lambda$ = the largest eigenvalue found by the outer loop) has the *same* eigenvectors as $A$ but with eigenvalues $\lambda-\lambda_i$, flipping the ordering: $A$'s smallest eigenvalue becomes $\lambda I-A$'s largest, so the ordinary power method (run as the inner loop on $y$) finds it.

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-04-ex10-smallest-eigenvalue" />

**Why care about eigenvalues/eigenvectors at all?** A short, concrete list, several of which you'll meet again directly in your own research: least-squares problems (finding the smallest eigenvalue/-vector, e.g. camera calibration, bundle adjustment, plane fitting), PCA, stationary distributions of Markov processes (directly relevant to MDPs and RL), PageRank, spectral clustering, and spectral learning methods (e.g. for training HMMs).

## Summary

- $A=USV^\top$ gives explicit orthonormal bases for the input space ($V$) and output space ($U$), with singular values $\sigma_i$ describing exactly how much each input direction is scaled on its way to the corresponding output direction; every matrix decomposes into $k=\text{rank}(A)$ rank-1 "project-scale-unproject" pieces.
- For symmetric $A$, the SVD and eigendecomposition coincide (up to sign): $Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$, $A=V\Lambda V^\top$.
- The pseudo-inverse $A^\dagger$ reverses the SVD's stretching; for full column rank it reduces to the normal-equations formula $(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$.
- A dataset's covariance matrix is symmetric PSD; its eigenvectors (sorted by eigenvalue) are PCA's principal directions, with eigenvalues equal to the variance along each.
- The eigendecomposition (Definition 3.18) is a definition, not a theorem, it doesn't always exist, but always does for symmetric matrices.
- The power method finds the largest eigenvalue/eigenvector iteratively via repeated multiplication and normalization; a variant using $\lambda I-A$ finds the smallest.
