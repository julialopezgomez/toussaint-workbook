## Why this matters

The SVD underlies PCA (dimensionality reduction), least-squares solutions, matrix conditioning/numerical stability, and low-rank approximation, all of which reappear throughout this course. It's also one of the cleanest illustrations of MATH-03's theme: a matrix looks arbitrary in the standard basis, but in the *right* basis (the singular directions), its action becomes as simple as possible: independent scalings along orthogonal axes.

## Matrices as row space / column space

A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ can be viewed as a collection of rows (each row dotted with $x$ picks up a component of $x$ within the row-spanned input space), or as a collection of columns (applying $A$ to $x$ linearly combines the columns, weighted by $x$'s entries, landing in the column-spanned output space). The problem: raw rows/columns are generally neither orthonormal nor guaranteed independent, so there's no clean, explicit description of exactly how $A$ transports the input space to the output space.

## The SVD theorem

For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, there exists a rank $k \le \min(m,n)$, orthonormal vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$, orthonormal vectors $u_1,\dots,u_k\in\mathbb{R}^m$, and scalars $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_k > 0$, such that

$$A = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top = USV^\top, \qquad S=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1:k}),\ U=u_{1:k},\ V=v_{1:k}$$

Each term $\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$ projects $x$ onto input direction $v_i$, scales by $\sigma_i$, and places the result along output direction $u_i$, entirely independently of the other terms (since the $v_i$ are mutually orthogonal and the $u_i$ are mutually orthogonal).

## Computing an SVD by hand: the eigendecomposition connection

You rarely need to invoke specialized SVD algorithms for small matrices: the SVD is directly computable from an eigendecomposition you may already know how to do.

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-04-ex2-eigendecomp-connection" />

## Worked example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$ (symmetric, chosen so the eigendecomposition is clean). Since $A$ is symmetric, $A^\top A = A^2$, and $A$'s own eigendecomposition gives the SVD directly (up to sign): eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda=4$ (eigenvector $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$) and $\lambda=2$ (eigenvector $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$). Both eigenvalues are positive, so the singular values are just $\sigma_1=4$, $\sigma_2=2$ (for a symmetric positive-definite matrix, singular values equal eigenvalues exactly, and $U=V$).

*(Verified with NumPy's `svd` while authoring this module: $S=[4.0, 2.0]$, matching exactly.)*

## Summary

- $A=USV^\top$ gives explicit orthonormal bases for the input space ($V$) and output space ($U$), with singular values $\sigma_i$ describing exactly how much each input direction is scaled on its way to the corresponding output direction.
- $A^\top A = VS^2V^\top$: the SVD is directly computable from the eigendecomposition of $A^\top A$ (columns of $V$ = eigenvectors, $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$).
- Every matrix of rank $k$ decomposes into exactly $k$ rank-1 pieces ($\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$), the minimal atomic units of any linear map.
