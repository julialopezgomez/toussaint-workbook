## Why this matters

Positive (semi-)definiteness is one of the single most-used properties in this entire course: it's the condition for a stationary point being a genuine minimum (MATH-02's Hessian eigenvalues), the condition for a valid covariance matrix (PROB block), the condition for a valid kernel (GP regression, MATH-02B), and the condition that makes ridge regression, Gauss-Newton, and Newton's method all well-posed. Despite that ubiquity, the source only ever *uses* the term "positive definite" (in Definition 3.10, defining a scalar product) without a single dedicated, spelled-out Definition box for what it means for a general matrix. This module fills that gap explicitly, right where the concept first becomes unavoidable.

## The scalar product

So far, everything in [MATH-03](/course/MATH/MATH-03) (bases, coordinates, dual spaces, matrices) never referred to a scalar product or a transpose at all, those concepts were fully independent of any notion of "length" or "angle." Now we introduce one:

> **Definition 3.10 (scalar product).** A scalar product (also called **inner product**) of $V$ is a symmetric, positive definite 2-form
> $$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle: V\times V\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad \langle v,w\rangle=\langle w,v\rangle, \qquad \langle v,v\rangle>0 \;\;\forall v\neq0\in V$$

This packs together two separate requirements, both worth unpacking on their own since they recur constantly outside this specific context:

> **Definition (positive semi-definite / positive definite matrix).** A symmetric matrix $M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is:
> - **positive semi-definite (PSD)**, written $M\succeq0$, if $v^\top Mv \geq 0$ for **all** $v\in\mathbb{R}^n$;
> - **positive definite (PD)**, written $M\succ0$, if $v^\top Mv > 0$ for **all** $v\neq0$.
>
> Equivalently (a fact you'll prove in [MATH-05](/course/MATH/MATH-05) via the eigendecomposition $M=\sum_i\lambda_ih_ih_i^\top$ from MATH-02): $M\succeq0$ iff all its eigenvalues $\lambda_i\geq0$; $M\succ0$ iff all $\lambda_i>0$.

So Definition 3.10's requirement "$\langle v,v\rangle>0\;\forall v\neq0$" is *exactly* saying: whatever matrix $G$ represents this scalar product in a chosen basis (formalized below as the metric tensor) must be positive definite. This is not a coincidental overlap in terminology, a scalar product **is**, by definition, "a PD matrix, viewed as a 2-form."

A fast practical check for small matrices, worth having on hand: **Sylvester's criterion** says a symmetric matrix is PD iff all its leading principal minors (determinants of the top-left $k\times k$ submatrices, for $k=1,..,n$) are positive. For a $2\times2$ symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}$, this reduces to: $a>0$ and $ad-b^2>0$.

## The metric tensor

Given a scalar product and a basis $(e_i)_{i=1}^n$, we can express $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ in coordinates:

> **Definition 3.13 (metric tensor).** Given a scalar product and basis $(e_i)_{i=1}^n$, the metric tensor is $g_{ij}=\langle e_i,e_j\rangle$, the coordinates of the 2-form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$. This implies
> $$\langle v,w\rangle = \sum_{ij}v_iw_j\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = \sum_{ij}v_iw_jg_{ij} = v^\top Gw$$

(Don't confuse $g_{ij}$ with the unrelated notion of a metric $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ in a metric space, same word, different concept, despite the name.) $G$ must itself be symmetric positive definite (inheriting Definition 3.10's requirements directly), so everything above about PD matrices applies to $G$ specifically.

## Orthonormal bases: where everything simplifies

> **Definition 3.12 (orthonormal).** Given a scalar product, a set of vectors $\{v_i\}_{i=1}^n$ is called orthonormal iff $\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$.

If the basis $(e_i)$ is itself orthonormal, the metric tensor collapses to the identity, $g_{ij}=\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}=I$ (this special case is called a **Euclidean** metric), so $\langle v,w\rangle = v^\top w$, the ordinary dot product, no $G$ needed. This is *why* the ordinary dot product "just works" as an inner product for the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$: it's the orthonormal special case of the general $v^\top Gw$ formula, not a separate, more fundamental notion.

A second important simplification: for an orthonormal basis, the scalar-product-induced dual vector $e_i^*=\langle e_i,\cdot\rangle$ (Definition 3.11) becomes **identical** to the purely coordinate-based dual basis $\hat e_i$ from [MATH-03](/course/MATH/MATH-03) (which needed no scalar product at all to define). In general, these two "dual" notions are different 1-forms; orthonormality is exactly the condition that collapses them into one.

This also gives a clean formula for **orthogonal projection**. The projection of $x$ onto a single orthonormal basis vector $e_i$ is $e_i\langle e_i,x\rangle$; the projection onto the span of several orthonormal vectors $(e_1,..,e_k)$ is

$$P_{\text{span}(e_1,..,e_k)}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^ke_i\langle e_i,x\rangle$$

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-03B-ex3-projection-derivation" />

Finally, note $v^*(w)=\langle v,w\rangle=v^\top w$ for an orthonormal basis: $v^\top$ really is the coordinate representation of the 1-form $v^*=\langle v,\cdot\rangle$, tying together the "row vector eats a vector, outputs a scalar, therefore a 1-form" intuition from MATH-03 with the scalar product concretely, for the first time using a transpose in a way that *does* encode a genuine scalar product (rather than just "undoing" a definitional transpose, as in the gradient notation of MATH-02).

## Summary

- A scalar product (Definition 3.10) is a symmetric, positive-definite 2-form, i.e. exactly "a PD matrix, viewed as a bilinear form."
- $M\succeq0$ (PSD) means $v^\top Mv\geq0\;\forall v$; $M\succ0$ (PD) means $v^\top Mv>0\;\forall v\neq0$; both are equivalent to conditions on $M$'s eigenvalues. Sylvester's criterion gives a fast check via leading principal minors.
- The metric tensor $g_{ij}=\langle e_i,e_j\rangle$ encodes the scalar product in coordinates: $\langle v,w\rangle=v^\top Gw$.
- For an orthonormal basis, $G=I$ (the Euclidean case), the scalar product reduces to the ordinary dot product, and orthogonal projection onto a span of orthonormal vectors is $\sum_ie_i\langle e_i,x\rangle$.
