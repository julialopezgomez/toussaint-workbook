## Why this matters

Every gradient you'll ever compute, whether it's a robot dynamics Jacobian, a loss function's gradient in a learning algorithm, or a trajectory optimizer's cost gradient, is built out of the chain rule applied systematically. This module rebuilds that foundation from scratch, including the notation and formal definitions the rest of this course leans on constantly, so you never have to stop and wonder "wait, what does this symbol mean again" mid-derivation.

## Notation conventions

Toussaint's notes open with a short but important chapter on notation, worth internalizing now rather than guessing later. A handful of conventions recur throughout this whole course:

- $[a=b] \in \{0,1\}$ is the **indicator function** (also written $\delta_{ab}$, the **Kronecker delta**): 1 if $a=b$, else 0.
- The **$p$-norm** is $\|x\|_p = \left(\sum_i |x_i|^p\right)^{1/p}$. By default, $\|x\| = \|x\|_2$, the ordinary **Euclidean length**. The **$L^1$-norm** $\|x\|_1 = \sum_i|x_i|$ (Manhattan distance) also plays an important role, especially in sparsity-inducing optimization.
- $\text{diag}(a_1,..,a_n)$ is the diagonal matrix with $a_1,..,a_n$ on the diagonal; conversely $\text{diag}(A) = (A_{11},..,A_{nn})$ extracts the diagonal of a square matrix $A$.
- $\mathbf{0}_n, \mathbf{1}_n \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are the all-zeros/all-ones vectors; $\mathbf{I}_n = \text{diag}(\mathbf{1}_n)$ is the identity matrix; $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector (all zeros except a 1 in position $i$).
- $A \circ B$ is the **Hadamard product** (elementwise product) of two matrices or vectors, the same $\odot$ notation you saw in ML-03 and KIN-02.

**Type-checking discipline**: within any equation, always know exactly what type each term is (scalar? vector? matrix? tensor?) and whether both sides agree in type and dimension. This sounds trivial but is the single most common source of bugs in hand-derived equations, check it explicitly, every time, especially once matrix calculus (MATH-02B) gets involved.

## Continuity, differentiability, smoothness

Formal definitions worth having precisely, not just "vaguely remembered":

- $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is **continuous** at $x$ if $\lim_{h\to0}f(x+h) = f(x)$ (the limit exists and equals $f(x)$, from both sides).
- $f$ is **differentiable** at $x$ if $f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ exists. Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous (but not conversely, e.g. $|x|$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$).
- $f$ is $\mathcal{C}^k$ if it's $k$-fold continuously differentiable ($\mathcal{C}^0$ = continuous, $\mathcal{C}^2$ = twice continuously differentiable, the level Hessian-based methods in MATH-02/OPT-01 generally assume).
- $f \in \mathcal{C}^\infty$ is called **smooth** (infinitely differentiable). Polynomials, $\sin$, $\cos$, $\exp$ are all smooth; a piecewise-linear function is continuous but not differentiable at the "kinks."

## Basis functions, briefly

A **polynomial** of degree $p$ is a weighted sum of monomials, $f(x) = \sum_{i=0}^p a_ix^i$. More generally, a set of **basis functions** $\{e_1,..,e_n\}$ defines the space of all their linear combinations, $f = \sum_i \alpha_i e_i$. Common choices beyond monomials: trigonometric functions (the Fourier basis), and **radial basis functions** $\varphi(|x-c_i|)$ centered at points $c_i$, a very common one being the squared exponential $\varphi(d)=\exp(-ld^2)$ (a non-normalized Gaussian bell curve, which you'll meet properly in PROB-05). **Splines** (KIN's motion-primitive module territory, and Toussaint's own `splines` note) are piecewise polynomials, continuously joined; a spline of degree $p$ is $\mathcal{C}^{p-1}$, one derivative less smooth than a true degree-$p$ polynomial, because the piece boundaries limit how many derivatives can be guaranteed continuous.

## Partial vs. total derivatives

Consider $z = f(x,y)$, a function of two variables. Formally:

> **Definition (partial derivative).** The partial derivative of $f(x_1,..,x_n)$ w.r.t. one argument is the ordinary derivative treating every other argument as fixed: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_i}f(x_1,..,x_n) = \lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x_1,..,x_i+h,..,x_n)-f(x_1,..,x_n)}{h}$.

Now suppose $x$ and $y$ *aren't* actually independent: both depend on a third variable, $x=x(t)$, $y=y(t)$. The **total derivative** $dz/dt$ answers a different question: if I nudge $t$, how does $z$ actually change, given that nudging $t$ moves both $x$ and $y$ at once? The bottom line: the partial derivative depends entirely on what you explicitly declared as the function's arguments, if you suddenly treat $z$ as depending on $t$ through both $x(t)$ and $y(t)$, "the derivative of $z$" is genuinely ambiguous until you say which one you mean.

## Computation graphs and the two chain rules

To handle dependency chains of arbitrary depth cleanly, Toussaint introduces a formal structure:

> **Definition (computation graph / function network).** A directed acyclic graph (DAG) of $n$ quantities $x_i$, where each is a deterministic function of a set of parents $\pi(i)\subset\{1,..,n\}$: $x_i = f_i(x_{\pi(i)})$.

Given a small variation of an input, how do all downstream values vary? It turns out there are **two** equivalent chain rules, depending on which direction you propagate:

> **Forward-version chain rule:** $\dfrac{df}{dx} = \displaystyle\sum_{g\in\pi(f)} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial g}\dfrac{dg}{dx}$ (with $dx/dx\equiv1$, in case $x\in\pi(f)$).
>
> Read as: "the change of $f$ with $x$ is the sum of changes that come from its direct dependence on each $g\in\pi(f)$, each multiplied by the change of that $g$ with $x$."
>
> **Backward-version chain rule:** $\dfrac{df}{dx} = \displaystyle\sum_{g:\,x\in\pi(g)} \dfrac{df}{dg}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}$ (with $df/df\equiv1$).
>
> Read as: "the change of $f$ with $x$ is the sum of changes that arise from all $g$ which directly depend on $x$."

If you recursively expand either one, they yield the *same* expression in terms of partial derivatives only, they're two equivalent ways of walking the same graph. The backward version is exactly what backprop does (you met this concretely in ML-03): propagate gradients from $f$ down toward $x$, one step at a time.

(It should reduce to just $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}$, since $dy/dt=0$ kills the second term.)

## Worked example

Let $z = x^2y + \sin(x)$, with $x=t^2$, $y=t+1$. Compute $dz/dt$ at $t=1$.

**Direct substitution check first** (so we have a ground truth to verify against): $z(t) = t^4(t+1) + \sin(t^2) = t^5+t^4+\sin(t^2)$, so $dz/dt = 5t^4+4t^3+2t\cos(t^2)$. At $t=1$: $5+4+2\cos(1) = 9+2\cos(1) \approx 10.081$.

**Via the chain rule**: $\partial z/\partial x = 2xy+\cos(x)$, $\partial z/\partial y = x^2$. At $t=1$: $x=1, y=2$, so $\partial z/\partial x = 2(1)(2)+\cos(1) = 4+\cos(1)$, $\partial z/\partial y = 1$. Also $dx/dt=2t=2$, $dy/dt=1$ at $t=1$.

$$\frac{dz}{dt}\bigg|_{t=1} = (4+\cos(1))(2) + (1)(1) = 8+2\cos(1)+1 = 9+2\cos(1) \approx 10.081$$

Matches the direct computation exactly, as it must.

## Multiple paths: the computation-graph view

When $z$ depends on $t$ through *more than one* intermediate variable, each path from $t$ to $z$ contributes its own term, and the total derivative sums over all paths. This is exactly the forward-version chain rule above, applied when a node has multiple parents.

## Summary

- Core notation: $[a=b]$/Kronecker delta, $p$-norms, $\text{diag}(\cdot)$, Hadamard product $\circ$, standard basis vectors $e_i$.
- Continuity $\Leftarrow$ differentiability $\Leftarrow$ smoothness ($\mathcal{C}^\infty$); these are a strict chain of implications, not equivalent.
- $\partial f/\partial x$ isolates one direct effect; $dz/dt$ sums *all* the indirect effects of $t$ on $z$, through every variable that depends on $t$.
- A computation graph formalizes dependency chains; the forward and backward chain rules are two equivalent ways to compute a total derivative by walking that graph, backprop is the backward version.
