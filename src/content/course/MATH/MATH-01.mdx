## Why this matters

Every gradient you'll ever compute, whether it's a robot dynamics Jacobian, a loss function's gradient in a learning algorithm, or a trajectory optimizer's cost gradient, is built out of the chain rule applied systematically. This module rebuilds that foundation from scratch: not just recalling the chain rule formula, but understanding *why* it's true, so you can apply it correctly even when a problem doesn't look like a textbook example.

## Partial vs. total derivatives

Consider $z = f(x,y)$, a function of two variables. The **partial derivative** $\partial f/\partial x$ answers: if I nudge $x$ alone, holding $y$ fixed, how does $z$ change? It treats $x$ and $y$ as independent.

Now suppose $x$ and $y$ *aren't* actually independent: both depend on a third variable, $x=x(t)$, $y=y(t)$. The **total derivative** $dz/dt$ answers a different question: if I nudge $t$, how does $z$ actually change, given that nudging $t$ moves both $x$ and $y$ at once?

## Deriving the chain rule

The total derivative isn't a new kind of derivative, it's built entirely out of partial derivatives, combined via the chain rule:

$$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$$

This is worth deriving properly once, rather than just memorizing, because the *reasoning* (a first-order Taylor expansion) is exactly the reasoning you'll reuse for every multivariable derivative in this course.

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-01-ex2-chainrule-derivation" />

**Stop and try**: before continuing, check that the formula degenerates correctly in a trivial case: if $y$ doesn't actually depend on $t$ at all (i.e. $y$ is a constant), what does $dz/dt$ reduce to? (It should reduce to just $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}$, since $dy/dt=0$ kills the second term.)

## Worked example

Let $z = x^2y + \sin(x)$, with $x=t^2$, $y=t+1$. Compute $dz/dt$ at $t=1$.

**Direct substitution check first** (so we have a ground truth to verify against): $z(t) = t^4(t+1) + \sin(t^2) = t^5+t^4+\sin(t^2)$, so $dz/dt = 5t^4+4t^3+2t\cos(t^2)$. At $t=1$: $5+4+2\cos(1) = 9+2\cos(1) \approx 10.081$.

**Via the chain rule**: $\partial z/\partial x = 2xy+\cos(x)$, $\partial z/\partial y = x^2$. At $t=1$: $x=1, y=2$, so $\partial z/\partial x = 2(1)(2)+\cos(1) = 4+\cos(1)$, $\partial z/\partial y = 1$. Also $dx/dt=2t=2$, $dy/dt=1$ at $t=1$.

$$\frac{dz}{dt}\bigg|_{t=1} = (4+\cos(1))(2) + (1)(1) = 8+2\cos(1)+1 = 9+2\cos(1) \approx 10.081$$

Matches the direct computation exactly, as it must.

## Multiple paths: the computation-graph view

When $z$ depends on $t$ through *more than one* intermediate variable, each path from $t$ to $z$ contributes its own term, and the total derivative sums over all paths. This is the computation-graph picture you'll use constantly from here on: draw $t \to x \to z$ and $t \to y \to z$ as two separate arrows-chains, and the chain rule is just "multiply along each chain, then add the chains up."

## Summary

- $\partial f/\partial x$ isolates one direct effect; $dz/dt$ sums *all* the indirect effects of $t$ on $z$, through every variable that depends on $t$.
- The chain rule formula follows directly from a first-order Taylor expansion of $f$, it's not a separate rule to memorize independently of Taylor expansions.
- When multiple paths connect a variable to the output, sum the chain-rule contribution of each path.
