## Why this matters

This is a short module, but it directly sets up the Optimization block: several "improved" gradient-descent variants (natural gradient, Gauss-Newton's implicit metric, even preconditioned gradient descent) are all instances of exactly this idea, replacing the plain gradient with a metric-corrected version. Understanding *why* the correction is needed, not just the formula, will make those later variants feel like the same idea applied again rather than a new trick each time.

## Prerequisites & readiness check

You should be comfortable with gradients (MATH-02) and basic constrained-optimization intuition (Lagrange multipliers, if you've seen them before; this module reintroduces the mechanics needed).

## The differential as a 1-form, formally

Recall from [MATH-03](/course/MATH/MATH-03) that a derivative is naturally a 1-form (a row-convention object that "eats" a tangent-step vector and outputs a scalar change). The source states this precisely:

> **Definition 3.19 (differential).** Given $f:V\to G$, the differential is $df|_x: V\to G,\; v\mapsto\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x+hv)-f(x)}{h}$.

$df|_x$ is linear in $v$ (the directional derivative), so $df|_x$ is a **$G$-valued 1-form**. What we usually call "the gradient" or "the Jacobian" is simply the *coordinates* of this 1-form in a chosen basis, exactly MATH-02's row-vector convention, now with its formal justification: a gradient is co-variant (transforms like a 1-form) precisely because it *is* one.

## Deriving the covariant gradient

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-05-ex2-steepest-descent-derivation" />

The direction $-G^{-1}\nabla f(x)$ is called the **covariant gradient**: it's the plain gradient, corrected by the inverse metric, to give the direction that's actually steepest under whatever notion of "distance" $G$ encodes. When $G=I$ (Euclidean metric), this reduces exactly to the plain gradient, consistent with the ordinary case being a special case of the general one.

## Worked example

At a point where $\nabla f(x) = \begin{pmatrix}6\\13\end{pmatrix}$ (reusing the gradient computed in MATH-02's worked example), suppose the metric is $G=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}$ (representing, say, a rescaling where the first coordinate's units cost twice as much "distance" per unit as Euclidean, and the second costs half).

$$G^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad d = -G^{-1}\nabla f(x) = -\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}6\\13\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-3\\-26\end{pmatrix}$$

Compare to the plain gradient descent direction, $-\nabla f(x) = \begin{pmatrix}-6\\-13\end{pmatrix}$: the covariant direction moves *less* in the first coordinate (which is "expensive" under $G$) and *more* in the second (which is "cheap"), exactly compensating for the metric.

*(Verified numerically while authoring this module: a random search over 200,000 candidate directions of fixed $G$-length confirmed $-G^{-1}\nabla f(x)$, normalized, is indeed the direction minimizing $\nabla f(x)^\top d$ among them, matching the analytic formula to 3 decimal places.)*

## Summary

- "Steepest descent" is only well-defined relative to a chosen metric $G$; the plain gradient is steepest specifically under the Euclidean metric $G=I$.
- The general steepest-descent direction is $-G^{-1}\nabla f(x)$ (the covariant gradient), derived via a Lagrange-multiplier argument minimizing $\nabla f(x)^\top d$ subject to $d^\top Gd=1$.
- This idea reappears throughout the Optimization block under different names (natural gradient, preconditioning, the implicit metric in Gauss-Newton).
