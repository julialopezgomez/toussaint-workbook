## The Jacobian and the gradient

> **Definition 2.3 (Jacobian).** Given $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^d$, the derivative (Jacobian matrix) is
> $$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x) = \begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}f_1(x) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2}f_1(x) & \cdots & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_n}f_1(x)\\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}f_2(x) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2}f_2(x) & \cdots & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_n}f_2(x)\\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}f_d(x) & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_2}f_d(x) & \cdots & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_n}f_d(x)\end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$$
> i.e. row $i$, column $j$ holds $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$: one row per output, one column per input.

> **Definition 2.4 (gradient).** When $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ has a single output, the Jacobian is a row vector, $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x) = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}f,\,..,\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_n}f\right)$. We define the **gradient** as the corresponding column vector: $\nabla f(x) = \left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x)\right]^\top$.

This row-vector-vs-column-vector distinction (Jacobian = row convention, gradient = column convention) is a deliberate, consistent choice in this course (matching contravariance, which you'll meet properly once tensors come up), not a typo, keep it straight since mixing the two conventions mid-derivation is a common source of shape errors.

The "purpose" of a derivative is to predict a change in function value from a change in input, to first order:

$$f(x+\delta) - f(x) \doteq \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x}\,\delta = \nabla f(x)^\top\delta$$

where $\doteq$ means "equal in first-order approximation." This holds regardless of whether the output space is $\mathbb{R}^d$ or $\mathbb{R}$, and regardless of whether $\delta\in\mathbb{R}^n$ or $\delta\in\mathbb{R}$.

Given a unit direction $d$ (i.e. $\|d\|=1$), the **directional derivative** of $f$ along $d$ is $\nabla f(x)^\top d$, and it holds that

$$\nabla f(x)^\top d = \lim_{\epsilon\to0}\frac{f(x+\epsilon d)-f(x)}{\epsilon}$$

i.e. the gradient tells you, in a single vector, the instantaneous rate of change along *any* direction: just dot it with that direction.

## The Hessian, and what its eigenvalues mean

> **Definition 2.5 (Hessian).** For a scalar function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, the Hessian is the symmetric matrix of second partial derivatives:
> $$\nabla^2f(x) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\nabla f(x) = \begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_1\partial x_1}f & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_1\partial x_n}f\\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_n\partial x_1}f & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_n\partial x_n}f\end{pmatrix}$$

The Hessian can be thought of as "the Jacobian of $\nabla f$." For a univariate function, the Hessian is just a single number, the second derivative $f''(x)$ (called the function's "curvature" in this context): $f''(x)>0$ means locally curved upward (**convex**), $f''(x)<0$ means locally curved downward (**concave**).

In the multivariate case, since $H$ is symmetric, it can always be decomposed as $H=\sum_i\lambda_ih_ih_i^\top$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and eigenvectors $h_i$ that are all mutually orthogonal, forming an orthonormal basis (you'll prove this general fact about symmetric matrices in [MATH-05](/course/MATH/MATH-05)). This gives a strong geometric picture of **how $H$ describes local curvature**: $\lambda_i$ is exactly the directional curvature along eigenvector $h_i$. If $\lambda_i>0$, $f$ curves upward along $h_i$; if $\lambda_i<0$, it curves downward along $h_i$.

This becomes especially useful where $\nabla f(x)=0$ (a **stationary point**, zero slope in every direction):

- If **all** $\lambda_i>0$ (H is positive definite), $f$ is locally convex in every direction and $x$ is a **local minimum**.
- If **all** $\lambda_i<0$ (H is negative definite), $f$ is locally concave in every direction and $x$ is a **local maximum**.
- If some $\lambda_i>0$ and some $\lambda_i<0$, $f$ curves down in some directions and up in others: $x$ is a **saddle point** (a local min along some directions, local max along others).

This is exactly why second-order optimization methods (Newton's method, in [OPT-02](/course/OPT/OPT-02)) examine the Hessian's eigenvalues, and why "positive semi-definite" as a property of a matrix (formalized properly once we reach scalar products, [MATH-03B](/course/MATH/MATH-03B)) is such a central concept in optimization: it's precisely the algebraic condition for "this stationary point is a genuine (local) minimum, not a saddle."

## Taylor expansion

The multivariable second-order Taylor expansion is the direct generalization of what you derived from scratch in [MATH-00](/course/MATH/MATH-00) for a single variable: $f(x+v) \approx f(x)+f'(x)v+\tfrac12f''(x)v^2+\cdots$ becomes, for $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$,

$$f(x+v) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top v + \frac12 v^\top\nabla^2f(x)\,v + \cdots$$

or, written out in index notation to make every shape explicit,

$$f(x+v) \approx f(x) + \sum_j\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}f(x)\,v_j + \frac12\sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_j\partial x_k}f(x)\,v_jv_k + \cdots$$

The scalar $f'(x)\delta$ becomes $\nabla f(x)^\top\delta$ (dot the gradient with the step direction), and $\tfrac12f''(x)\delta^2$ becomes $\tfrac12\delta^\top H(x)\delta$ (sandwich the step direction between the Hessian). You'll use this constantly throughout the Optimization block.

## Checking your gradients numerically

Deriving a gradient by hand is error-prone, and a subtly wrong gradient can make an optimizer silently converge to the wrong place. The standard defense: compare your analytical gradient/Jacobian against a **finite-difference** approximation, which estimates the derivative directly from the definition, no calculus required. This is described in the source as the actual, standard workflow for implementing any ML/AI/optimization method: (1) formalize the objective on paper, (2) derive gradients/Hessian on paper, (3) implement both the objective and the analytic gradient in code, (4) **test the implementation against a finite-difference estimate**, (5) only then plug it into an optimizer.

```
Algorithm 1: Finite Difference Jacobian Check
Input:    x ∈ ℝⁿ, function f: ℝⁿ→ℝᵈ, function df: ℝⁿ→ℝᵈˣⁿ
1: initialize Ĵ ∈ ℝᵈˣⁿ, and ε = 10⁻⁶
2: for i = 1 : n do
3:   Ĵ.,i = [f(x+ε·eᵢ) - f(x-ε·eᵢ)] / 2ε          // assigns the i-th column of Ĵ
4: end for
5: if ‖Ĵ - df(x)‖∞ < 10⁻⁴ return true; else false
```
*(Marc Toussaint, `lecture-maths.pdf`, p.18. Here eᵢ is the i-th standard basis vector in ℝⁿ.)*

Two details worth noticing: this uses the **centered** difference $[f(x+\epsilon e_i)-f(x-\epsilon e_i)]/2\epsilon$ rather than the one-sided $[f(x+\epsilon e_i)-f(x)]/\epsilon$ you may have seen elsewhere, centered differences have error $O(\epsilon^2)$ instead of $O(\epsilon)$, so they're the right default. And the check is column-by-column: perturbing input $i$ alone (via $\epsilon e_i$) estimates exactly column $i$ of the Jacobian, $\partial f/\partial x_i$, matching Definition 2.3 directly.

## The gradient of a quadratic form

Quadratic forms $f(x) = x^\top Ax$ show up constantly (cost functions, energy functions, the core of least-squares). Rather than memorizing $\nabla f(x) = (A+A^\top)x$, derive it once from index notation, so you can rederive it (or a variant, like a weighted quadratic form) whenever you need to.

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-02-ex2-quadratic-gradient-derivation" />

**Stop and try**: before continuing, check the formula's sanity for a symmetric $A$ ($A=A^\top$): it should simplify to $\nabla f(x) = 2Ax$. Confirm that follows directly from what you just derived.

## Worked example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ (not symmetric), $x=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$. Then $A+A^\top = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&6\end{pmatrix}$, and

$$\nabla f(x) = (A+A^\top)x = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&6\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}6\\13\end{pmatrix}$$

*(Independently verified with a finite-difference check at $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ while authoring this module: numerical gradient $[6.0, 13.0]$, matching exactly.)* The Hessian here is simply $A+A^\top = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&6\end{pmatrix}$ itself, since $f$ is quadratic (the gradient is linear in $x$, so its derivative, the Hessian, is constant). Its eigenvalues are both positive ($\lambda_1\approx3.30,\lambda_2\approx6.70$, you can verify via the characteristic polynomial from [MATH-05](/course/MATH/MATH-05)), confirming $x=0$ is this quadratic form's unique local (and here, global) minimum.

## Summary

- The Jacobian (Definition 2.3) is a matrix of $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$; the gradient (Definition 2.4) is its transpose for scalar-valued $f$, a deliberate row/column convention split.
- The directional derivative $\nabla f(x)^\top d$ gives the instantaneous rate of change along any unit direction $d$, and equals $\lim_{\epsilon\to0}[f(x+\epsilon d)-f(x)]/\epsilon$.
- The Hessian (Definition 2.5) is symmetric, hence orthogonally diagonalizable; its eigenvalues give the curvature along each corresponding eigenvector direction, which is how convex/concave/saddle stationary points are classified.
- $\nabla(x^\top Ax) = (A+A^\top)x$ in general, simplifying to $2Ax$ only when $A$ is symmetric, derive it from index notation rather than memorizing the symmetric-only shortcut.
- Always sanity-check a hand-derived gradient/Jacobian against a centered finite-difference numerical estimate (Algorithm 1) before trusting it, this is standard practice, not optional paranoia.
