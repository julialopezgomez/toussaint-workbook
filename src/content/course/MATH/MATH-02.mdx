Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians are the vocabulary every optimization algorithm and every learned model gradient in this course is written in. ## Gradient, Jacobian, Hessian

For a scalar-valued function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, the **gradient** $\nabla f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ collects all $n$ partial derivatives into a vector: $[\nabla f(x)]_i = \partial f/\partial x_i$.

For a vector-valued function $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, the **Jacobian** $J(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ generalizes this to a matrix: $J_{ij} = \partial f_i/\partial x_j$. (A gradient is just the special case $m=1$, written as a row or column depending on convention.)

The **Hessian** $H(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ collects second derivatives: $H_{ij} = \partial^2 f/\partial x_i \partial x_j$. It's symmetric whenever $f$ is twice continuously differentiable (mixed partials commute).

These feed directly into the **second-order Taylor expansion**, the direct multivariable generalization of what you derived from scratch in [MATH-00](/course/MATH/MATH-00): the scalar $f'(x)\delta$ becomes $\nabla f(x)^\top\delta$ (dot the gradient with the step direction), and $\tfrac12 f''(x)\delta^2$ becomes $\tfrac12 \delta^\top H(x)\delta$ (sandwich the step direction between the Hessian). You'll use this constantly throughout the Optimization block:

$$f(x+\delta) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top \delta + \tfrac12 \delta^\top H(x) \delta$$

## Checking your gradients numerically

Deriving a gradient by hand is error-prone, and a subtly wrong gradient can make an optimizer silently converge to the wrong place. The standard defense: compare your analytical gradient against a **finite-difference** approximation, which estimates the derivative directly from the definition, no calculus required:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \approx \frac{f(x+\epsilon e_i) - f(x-\epsilon e_i)}{2\epsilon}, \qquad \epsilon \approx 10^{-6}$$

## The gradient of a quadratic form

Quadratic forms $f(x) = x^\top Ax$ show up constantly (cost functions, energy functions, the core of least-squares). Rather than memorizing $\nabla f(x) = (A+A^\top)x$, derive it once from index notation, so you can rederive it (or a variant, like a weighted quadratic form) whenever you need to.

<ExerciseCard id="MATH-02-ex2-quadratic-gradient-derivation" />

**Stop and try**: before continuing, check the formula's sanity for a symmetric $A$ ($A=A^\top$): it should simplify to $\nabla f(x) = 2Ax$. Confirm that follows directly from what you just derived.

## Worked example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ (not symmetric), $x=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}$. Then $A+A^\top = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&6\end{pmatrix}$, and

$$\nabla f(x) = (A+A^\top)x = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&6\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}6\\13\end{pmatrix}$$

*(Independently verified with a finite-difference check at $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ while authoring this module: numerical gradient $[6.0, 13.0]$, matching exactly.)* The Hessian here is simply $A+A^\top = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&6\end{pmatrix}$ itself, since $f$ is quadratic (the gradient is linear in $x$, so its derivative, the Hessian, is constant).

## Summary

- Gradient (vector), Jacobian (matrix, vector-valued generalization of gradient), and Hessian (matrix of second derivatives) are all built from the same partial-derivative bookkeeping.
- $\nabla(x^\top Ax) = (A+A^\top)x$ in general, simplifying to $2Ax$ only when $A$ is symmetric, derive it from index notation rather than memorizing the symmetric-only shortcut.
- Always sanity-check a hand-derived gradient against a finite-difference numerical estimate before trusting it.
