## Why this matters

Every linearized dynamical system you'll analyze in Block DYN (robot dynamics near an equilibrium, a controller's closed-loop behavior) reduces to exactly this: a linear system $\dot x=Ax$, whose entire qualitative behavior (does it settle down? oscillate? blow up?) is read directly off $A$'s eigenvalues. This module is the direct payoff of MATH-04's eigendecomposition work, and it will not feel like new machinery, just a new place to point machinery you already have.

## From scalar to vector: $\dot x = Ax$

ODE-01 solved the scalar equation $\dot x=-kx$, with solution $x(t)=x_0e^{-kt}$. The natural generalization to a **system** of coupled linear ODEs, $x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\dot x = Ax, \qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\text{ constant}$$

e.g. $\dot x_1 = 2x_1 - x_2$, $\dot x_2 = x_1 + 3x_2$ is such a system, written compactly with $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$. Guessing the scalar solution's shape directly, try $x(t) = e^{\lambda t}v$ for a constant scalar $\lambda$ and constant vector $v$:

<ExerciseCard id="ODE-02-ex1-eigenvector-ansatz" />

Substituting this ansatz into $\dot x=Ax$ gives exactly $Av=\lambda v$: **$v$ must be an eigenvector of $A$, with eigenvalue $\lambda$**. This is the entire idea: every eigenpair $(\lambda_i,v_i)$ of $A$ gives one solution $e^{\lambda_it}v_i$, and (if $A$ is diagonalizable, with $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors) the **general solution** is a linear combination of all of them:

$$x(t) = \sum_{i=1}^nc_ie^{\lambda_it}v_i$$

with the coefficients $c_i$ fixed by the initial condition: $x(0)=\sum_ic_iv_i$, i.e. $c=V^{-1}x(0)$ where $V=[v_1\;\cdots\;v_n]$.

## Worked example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}$, $x(0)=\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalues from $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$: $(-2-\lambda)^2-1=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=-1$ or $\lambda=-3$. Eigenvectors: for $\lambda=-1$, $(A+I)v=0 \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}v=0 \Rightarrow v_1=(1,1)$; for $\lambda=-3$, $(A+3I)v=0 \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}v=0 \Rightarrow v_2=(1,-1)$.

Solving $c_1(1,1)+c_2(1,-1)=(3,1)$: $c_1+c_2=3$, $c_1-c_2=1$, giving $c_1=2,c_2=1$. So:

$$x(t) = 2e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} + e^{-3t}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2e^{-t}+e^{-3t}\\2e^{-t}-e^{-3t}\end{pmatrix}$$

*(Verified with a high-resolution RK4 numerical integration while authoring this module: the numerical trajectory at $t=1$ matched this closed form to 6 decimal places.)* Both eigenvalues are negative, so every term decays to zero: $x(t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$, regardless of the initial condition.

## Complex eigenvalues: oscillation

For a real matrix $A$, complex eigenvalues always come in conjugate pairs $\lambda=a\pm bi$. A clean special case, worth seeing directly rather than through the general complex-eigenvector machinery: $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, i.e. $\dot x_1=x_2$, $\dot x_2=-x_1$. Differentiating the first equation and substituting the second: $\ddot x_1 = \dot x_2 = -x_1$, the classic **harmonic oscillator** equation. With $x(0)=(1,0)$, the solution is $x_1(t)=\cos t$, $x_2(t)=\dot x_1(t) = -\sin t$.

*(Verified by direct substitution: $\ddot x_1 = -\cos t = -x_1$ ✓, and $\dot x_1=-\sin t=x_2$ ✓, both hold identically for all $t$.)* This matrix's eigenvalues are $\lambda=\pm i$ (purely imaginary, $a=0$): no growth or decay, pure rotation in the $(x_1,x_2)$-plane, exactly what $\cos t,-\sin t$ traces out. More generally, for $\lambda=a\pm bi$, the real part $a$ controls an $e^{at}$ growth/decay envelope and the imaginary part $b$ controls an oscillation at angular frequency $b$, combining into a **decaying spiral** ($a<0$), **growing spiral** ($a>0$), or a **pure rotation/center** ($a=0$, this example).

## Stability classification

Since $x(t)=\sum_ic_ie^{\lambda_it}v_i$, the long-term behavior of every solution is governed entirely by the eigenvalues' real parts:

> **Stability classification for $\dot x=Ax$.**
> - If $\text{Re}(\lambda_i)<0$ for **every** eigenvalue: every solution $\to0$ as $t\to\infty$, regardless of initial condition. Called **asymptotically stable**.
> - If $\text{Re}(\lambda_i)>0$ for **any** eigenvalue: solutions generically grow unboundedly. Called **unstable**.
> - If the largest real part is exactly $0$ (and no larger positive one exists): **marginally stable** (bounded but not decaying, e.g. the pure rotation above).

In 2D specifically, this gives a standard taxonomy worth recognizing by name (all classified by the pair of eigenvalues): both real negative → **stable node**; both real positive → **unstable node**; real with opposite signs → **saddle** (stable along one eigendirection, unstable along the other); complex with negative real part → **stable spiral**; complex with positive real part → **unstable spiral**; purely imaginary → **center**.

## Connection to robot dynamics: linearization

Real dynamics are rarely linear, $\dot x=f(x)$ for nonlinear $f$. But near an **equilibrium** $x^*$ (where $f(x^*)=0$), a first-order Taylor expansion ([MATH-00](/course/MATH/MATH-00)) gives $\dot x = f(x^*+\delta) \approx f(x^*) + \nabla f(x^*)\delta = \nabla f(x^*)\delta$ (writing $\delta=x-x^*$, the deviation from equilibrium), exactly the linear system $\dot\delta = A\delta$ with $A=\nabla f(x^*)$, the **Jacobian of the dynamics at the equilibrium**. This is why the eigenvalues of that Jacobian (not the original nonlinear $f$) are the standard local-stability test for a robot's equilibrium, controller fixed point, or any nonlinear system: locally, near $x^*$, the system behaves exactly like the linear system this module derives, and the stability classification above applies directly.

## Summary

- $\dot x=Ax$ has solutions built from $A$'s eigenpairs: $x(t)=\sum_ic_ie^{\lambda_it}v_i$, with $c=V^{-1}x(0)$.
- Complex eigenvalue pairs $\lambda=a\pm bi$ produce oscillation at frequency $b$ with growth/decay envelope $e^{at}$; purely imaginary eigenvalues give undamped rotation (the harmonic oscillator).
- Stability is read directly from the eigenvalues' real parts: all negative → asymptotically stable, any positive → unstable, largest exactly zero → marginally stable; the 2D taxonomy (node/saddle/spiral/center) names each qualitative case.
- Linearizing a nonlinear system $\dot x=f(x)$ at an equilibrium gives $\dot\delta=\nabla f(x^*)\delta$, so this entire module's machinery becomes the standard local-stability test for nonlinear dynamics, directly relevant to robot dynamics and control.
