## Definitions

- **Taylor expansion** (MATH-00): approximate $f$ near $x$ by matching derivatives with a polynomial; truncating after the linear term gives the tangent-line approximation, after the quadratic term the local bowl/saddle shape.
- **Partial vs. total derivative** (MATH-01): $\partial f/\partial x$ holds other variables fixed; $df/dt$ accounts for every variable's own dependence on $t$ via the chain rule.
- **Jacobian / gradient / Hessian** (MATH-02): the Jacobian is the first-derivative matrix of a vector-valued function; the gradient is the Jacobian of a scalar-valued function, transposed into a column vector; the Hessian is the second-derivative matrix, and equals the Jacobian of the gradient.
- **Vector space / dual space** (MATH-03): a vector $v$ is basis-independent; its coordinates $[v]_B$ depend on the chosen basis $B$. The dual space $V^*$ is the space of linear functionals on $V$; $\nabla f(x)$ naturally lives there, not in $V$ itself.
- **Metric tensor** (MATH-03B): $G$ (or $g_{ij}$) defines the inner product $\langle v,w\rangle = v^\top Gw$ in a given basis; $G=I$ is the Euclidean special case.
- **SVD / eigendecomposition** (MATH-04): every matrix decomposes into $k=\text{rank}(A)$ rank-1 "project–scale–unproject" pieces; for symmetric matrices, the SVD and eigendecomposition coincide.
- **Covariant gradient** (MATH-05): the metric-corrected steepest-descent direction, needed because $\nabla f(x)$ is a covector (dual-space object) but a descent direction must be an actual vector.

## Assumptions & applicability

- Taylor expansions are only valid **locally**: the approximation error grows with $\|\delta\|$ and with how non-polynomial $f$ actually is: don't trust a 2nd-order Taylor expansion far from the expansion point.
- The Hessian $H=J_{\nabla f}$ identity relies on **mixed partials commuting** ($\partial^2f/\partial x_i\partial x_j=\partial^2f/\partial x_j\partial x_i$), which holds for any twice-continuously-differentiable $f$ — true for essentially everything in this course, but not a universal law.
- $\nabla f(x)$ is a **covector**: treating it as an ordinary vector (i.e. using $G=I$ implicitly) is only correct under the Euclidean metric. Under any other metric, use the covariant gradient $-G^{-1}\nabla f(x)$ (MATH-05).
- Eigendecomposition and SVD **coincide only for symmetric matrices**; for a general (non-symmetric) matrix, always use the SVD, not the eigendecomposition, since eigenvectors of a non-symmetric matrix aren't generally orthogonal and eigenvalues can be complex.

## Common mistakes

- Writing $\nabla f(x)$ as a row vector out of habit from the Jacobian definition: by this course's convention, the gradient is a **column** vector, the transpose of the (row-vector) Jacobian of a scalar function.
- Forgetting the factor of $\tfrac12$ in the quadratic Taylor term, or in $\nabla_w(w^\top Aw)=(A+A^\top)w$ when $A$ isn't symmetric (the $A^\top$ term is easy to drop).
- Confusing a vector's **coordinates** (basis-dependent, a tuple of numbers) with the vector **itself** (basis-independent, MATH-03) — this is exactly why $\nabla f(x)$'s covector-vs-vector status matters at all.
- Assuming a matrix's eigenvalues are automatically its singular values: only true when the matrix is symmetric (or more generally normal); otherwise compute the SVD directly.
- Using plain gradient descent ($x\leftarrow x-\alpha\nabla f(x)$) under a non-Euclidean metric without correcting via $G^{-1}$ — silently uses the wrong descent direction (see MATH-EXAM's Part 1).

## Decision rule

- Need the fastest local decrease in $f$? Check whether your problem has a natural non-Euclidean metric (e.g. via a reparametrization or physical unit mismatch across coordinates); if so, use $-G^{-1}\nabla f(x)$, not $-\nabla f(x)$.
- Need to solve a least-squares-shaped objective (with or without a quadratic regularizer)? Expand it, apply $\nabla_w(a^\top w)=a$ and $\nabla_w(w^\top Aw)=(A+A^\top)w$ term by term, set the gradient to zero, solve the resulting linear system — don't re-derive from scratch each time.
- Given a matrix and unsure whether to eigendecompose or SVD it? If it's symmetric, either works (they coincide); otherwise, always SVD.
